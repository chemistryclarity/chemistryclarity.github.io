## A healing herb

*Centella asiatica* is a small creeping plant with fan-shaped leaves that grows in damp places across tropical Asia, Africa and Madagascar. It has long been used in **Ayurvedic** and **traditional Chinese medicine**, and in Madagascar, to help wounds heal and to treat skin conditions. Tigers are said to roll in it to heal their wounds, which is why it is sometimes called "tiger grass".

## From plant to medicine

In 1941 the French pharmacist **Jules Bontems** reported isolating the plant's active compounds, a family of **triterpene saponins**, starting with **asiaticoside**. In 1949 the Malagasy scientist **Albert Rakoto Ratsimamanga**, working with a French laboratory, developed **Madecassol**, a standardized Centella preparation for healing wounds and burns. The compound **madecassoside**, named after Madagascar, was later identified as one of the main active ingredients.

Madecassoside has two very different parts:

- a **triterpene** (madecassic acid): five fused carbon rings with 30 carbon atoms, mostly non-polar, like a fat;
- a chain of **three sugar units** with many –OH groups, very polar and water-loving.

The two are joined by an **ester** bond. A molecule with a non-polar part and a polar part is **amphiphilic**, like soap: saponins get their name from the Latin *sapo*, soap, because they foam when shaken with water.

<KeyIdea>
The sugar part makes the molecule more water-soluble and changes how it is absorbed, while the triterpene part carries much of the biological activity. Many natural products are **glycosides** built this way: salicin (in willow bark) is another example.
</KeyIdea>

## How it helps skin

Laboratory and animal studies suggest that madecassoside and its relatives stimulate skin cells to make **collagen**, reduce **inflammation**, and act as **antioxidants**, which together support wound healing. These properties explain its use in wound ointments and, more recently, in "cica" (from *Centella asiatica*) creams for sensitive or irritated skin.

<MorePrecisely>
The amount of madecassoside and asiaticoside in Centella varies with where and how the plant is grown, so modern products use standardized extracts measured by chromatography. Evidence for the cosmetic benefits comes mainly from laboratory and small clinical studies; as with all skin products, results vary from person to person.
</MorePrecisely>
