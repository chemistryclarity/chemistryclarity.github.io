## An old plant, a new medicine

Cannabis has been used in traditional medicine for thousands of years, but its chemistry was a mystery until the twentieth century. The plant makes more than a hundred related compounds called **cannabinoids**. In 1940 the American chemist **Roger Adams** isolated one of them, **cannabidiol** (CBD), from wild hemp, but he could not determine its complete structure.

In 1963 the Israeli chemist **Raphael Mechoulam** and his colleague Yuval Shvo, at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, published the structure of CBD, and in 1964 Mechoulam and Yehiel Gaoni isolated **THC** (Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol) and showed that it, not CBD, is responsible for the "high" of cannabis.

## Same formula, different molecules

<Figure src="/images/explore/structures/cannabidiol.svg" alt="Two structures with the same formula, C21H30O2, 314.5 g/mol. Δ9-Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), psychoactive: the same parts, but one OH has joined to the other ring, closing an extra ether ring containing oxygen." caption="CBD and THC are isomers: the same atoms, arranged differently. THC's extra ring (containing the ether oxygen) makes the molecule flatter and changes how it binds in the brain. Structures from PubChem." width={360} height={544} />

CBD and THC have exactly the same molecular formula, $\ce{C21H30O2}$: they are **isomers**. In CBD, the benzene ring carries **two –OH groups** and is joined to a separate six-membered ring. In THC, one of those –OH groups has formed an **ether** link to the other ring, closing an extra ring. This small change alters the molecule's shape and how it binds to cannabinoid receptors in the brain: THC strongly activates the CB1 receptor and is intoxicating, whereas CBD is not.

<KeyIdea>
Isomers can have completely different biological effects. Recognizing functional groups (here an alcohol versus an ether) helps explain why two molecules with the same formula behave so differently.
</KeyIdea>

## Epidiolex

Some children have rare, severe forms of epilepsy, such as **Dravet syndrome** and **Lennox–Gastaut syndrome**, in which seizures happen many times a day and standard medicines often fail. Reports from families using cannabis extracts led the British company GW Pharmaceuticals to develop **Epidiolex**, a purified, standardized CBD solution. In three randomized, placebo-controlled trials with more than 500 patients, it reduced seizure frequency. On **25 June 2018** the US FDA approved it for patients two years and older: the first FDA-approved medicine containing a purified substance from the cannabis plant.

<MorePrecisely>
How CBD reduces seizures is still not fully understood; it appears to act on several targets rather than one receptor. A prescription medicine differs from shop-bought CBD products: its purity and dose are controlled and tested, whereas the content of unregulated products can differ widely from the label. CBD can also interact with other epilepsy medicines, so it must be used under medical supervision. Laws on cannabis products differ from country to country.
</MorePrecisely>
