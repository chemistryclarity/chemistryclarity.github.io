## The carob tree

The carob tree grows around the Mediterranean, and its long brown pods have been eaten for thousands of years; the seeds were once used as standard weights for gold and gems, giving us the word **carat**. Today carob is best known as a chocolate substitute, but its pods also contain one of the richest natural supplies of **D-pinitol**.

## A sugar that is not a sugar

<Figure src="/images/explore/structures/pinitol.svg" alt="The structure of D-pinitol, C7H14O6, 194.18 g/mol, drawn without stereochemistry: a six-membered carbon ring in which every carbon carries an oxygen; five carry OH groups and one carries a methoxy group, OCH3." caption="D-Pinitol: a cyclohexane ring with five –OH groups and one –OCH₃ group. The exact 3D arrangement of the groups (D-chiro) is not shown. Structure from PubChem." width={360} height={354} />

Pinitol belongs to the **inositols** (cyclitols): six-membered carbon rings with an –OH group on every carbon. In pinitol, one –OH is replaced by a **methoxy** group (–OCH₃, an ether). With five –OH groups, pinitol forms many **hydrogen bonds** with water, so it is very soluble and tastes slightly sweet, like a sugar, although it is not a carbohydrate in the strict sense. The different inositols differ only in which –OH groups point up or down from the ring: they are **stereoisomers**, and the body can tell them apart.

<KeyIdea>
Small differences in 3D shape matter in biology. Pinitol is related to **D-chiro-inositol**, a molecule involved in how cells respond to insulin, and may be converted into it in the body. That connection is why researchers became interested in pinitol for blood-sugar control.
</KeyIdea>

## What the research says

Studies in diabetic animals found that pinitol lowered blood glucose and appeared to make cells more sensitive to insulin. Several small trials in people with insulin resistance, prediabetes or polycystic ovary syndrome have reported modest improvements in blood glucose or insulin sensitivity, and research on carob extracts continues.

<MorePrecisely>
Pinitol and carob extracts are sold as **dietary supplements**, not as approved medicines. The clinical studies so far are mostly small and short, and their results vary. Anyone with diabetes should not replace prescribed treatment with supplements, and should talk to a doctor before trying them. This story is a good example of how a natural product moves from traditional food to laboratory study to clinical trials, and why each step needs careful evidence.
</MorePrecisely>
