## Who she is

Tu Youyou was born in Ningbo, on China's east coast, in 1930. After recovering from tuberculosis as a teenager she decided to study medicine, and she graduated in pharmacy from Beijing Medical College in 1955. She has spent her whole career at what is now the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, studying the chemistry of traditional herbal medicines.

## The discovery of artemisinin

In 1969 Tu was appointed to lead a research group in **Project 523**, a secret national programme to find treatments for malaria, which had become resistant to the drug chloroquine. Her team collected more than 2000 traditional prescriptions and tested hundreds of plant extracts.

An extract of sweet wormwood (qinghao) was promising but unreliable. Reading a fourth-century text by Ge Hong, which advised soaking the herb in cold water rather than boiling it, Tu realized that **heat might be destroying the active compound**. She switched to extraction with diethyl ether at low temperature, and in 1971 obtained an extract that completely cleared the parasites in infected animals. In 1972 her team isolated the pure compound, **artemisinin**. Because there were no facilities for safety testing, Tu and two colleagues volunteered to take the extract themselves before it was given to patients.

Tu Youyou combined careful reading of **traditional medical texts** with modern **extraction, purification and testing**: an approach that shows how chemistry can turn traditional knowledge into a reliable medicine.
</KeyIdea>

## Prizes and legacy

Artemisinin-based combination therapies are now the standard treatment for malaria worldwide and have saved millions of lives. Tu received the **Lasker Award** in 2011 and, in 2015, half of the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**; the other half went to William C. Campbell and Satoshi Ōmura for avermectin, another natural-product drug, against parasitic worms. She became the first Chinese woman to win a Nobel Prize in a science, and is often described as a "three-noes" laureate: no doctorate, no study abroad, and no membership of the Chinese academies at the time of her discoveries.
