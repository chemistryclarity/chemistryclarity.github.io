## What is it?

**Chemical kinetics** is the study of how **fast** reactions happen. The **rate** of a reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time:

$$
\text{rate} = -\frac{\Delta[\text{reactant}]}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta[\text{product}]}{\Delta t}
$$

The minus sign makes the rate positive, because reactant concentrations decrease. Rates are usually given in **M/s** (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹).

<KeyIdea>
A **rate law** links the rate to the concentrations: rate $= k[\ce{A}]^m[\ce{B}]^n$. The orders $m$ and $n$ must be found **by experiment**; they are not the coefficients of the balanced equation.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Industry and safety.** Chemists control rates to make products efficiently and to avoid runaway reactions.
- **Medicine.** Drug doses are spaced using the rate at which the body removes the drug (often a first-order half-life).
- **Mechanisms.** Rate laws give clues about the steps by which a reaction actually happens.

## How does it work?

### 1. What changes the rate (collision theory)

Particles must **collide** with enough energy and the right orientation to react. A reaction is faster with:

- **higher concentration** (or pressure for gases): more frequent collisions;
- **higher temperature**: more frequent and, above all, more energetic collisions;
- **larger surface area** (smaller pieces of solid): more particles exposed;
- a **catalyst**: a lower-energy pathway (see [Activation Energy and Catalysts](/chemistry/activation-energy/)).

### 2. Rates and stoichiometry

For $\ce{2N2O5(g) -> 4NO2(g) + O2(g)}$, $\ce{NO2}$ forms twice as fast as $\ce{N2O5}$ disappears. To give one rate for the reaction, divide by the coefficients:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= -\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta[\ce{N2O5}]}{\Delta t} \\[4pt]
&= \frac{1}{4}\frac{\Delta[\ce{NO2}]}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta[\ce{O2}]}{\Delta t}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 3. Rate laws and orders

In rate $= k[\ce{A}]^m[\ce{B}]^n$:

- $m$ is the **order with respect to A**; $n$ is the order with respect to B; $m + n$ is the **overall order**.
- $k$ is the **rate constant**. It is fixed at a given temperature and increases as the temperature rises.

| Order in A | Doubling [A] multiplies the rate by |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1 (no change) |
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 |

### 4. Units of the rate constant

Because the rate is always in M/s, the units of $k$ depend on the **overall order**:

| Overall order | Rate law | Units of k |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | rate = k | M s⁻¹ |
| 1 | rate = k[A] | s⁻¹ |
| 2 | rate = k[A]² or k[A][B] | M⁻¹ s⁻¹ |
| 3 | rate = k[A]²[B] | M⁻² s⁻¹ |

### 5. First-order reactions and half-life

For a first-order reaction, the concentration falls **exponentially**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{A}]_t &= [\ce{A}]_0\, e^{-kt} \\[4pt]
t_{1/2} &= \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k}
\end{aligned}
$$

The **half-life**, $t_{1/2}$, is the time for the concentration to halve. For a first-order reaction it does **not** depend on the starting concentration, so every half-life is the same length.

<Analogy>
A ticket queue at a stadium: open more gates (higher concentration of "gates", or a catalyst) and people get through faster. A first-order half-life is like a sale where half of the remaining stock sells every hour: 100 items, then 50, then 25, then 12.5, with each halving taking the same time.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
The rate measured over a time interval is an **average rate**; the **instantaneous rate** is the slope of the tangent to the concentration–time curve. The orders in a rate law reflect the reaction **mechanism** (its sequence of elementary steps), which is why they can't be read from the overall equation. Second-order reactions have $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{1}{k[\ce{A}]_0}$, which does depend on the starting concentration.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Average rate">
**Question:** In $\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}$, $[\ce{H2O2}]$ falls from 0.500 M to 0.400 M in 60.0 s. Find the average rate of disappearance of $\ce{H2O2}$ and the average rate of the reaction.

1. Rate of disappearance of $\ce{H2O2}$:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &-\frac{\Delta[\ce{H2O2}]}{\Delta t} \\[4pt]
   &= -\frac{0.400\ \text{M} - 0.500\ \text{M}}{60.0\ \text{s}} \\[4pt]
   &= 1.67 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. Rate of reaction (divide by the coefficient 2): $\dfrac{1.67 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}}{2} =$ **$8.33 \times 10^{-4}$ M/s**
</Example>

<Example title="The method of initial rates">
**Question:** For $\ce{A + B -> products}$:

| Experiment | [A] | [B] | Initial rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.10 M | 0.10 M | 2.0 × 10⁻³ M/s |
| 2 | 0.20 M | 0.10 M | 8.0 × 10⁻³ M/s |
| 3 | 0.10 M | 0.20 M | 4.0 × 10⁻³ M/s |

Find the rate law and $k$, with units.

1. Experiments 1 → 2: [A] doubles, [B] constant; the rate is multiplied by $\dfrac{8.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}} = 4$, so the order in A is **2**.
2. Experiments 1 → 3: [B] doubles; the rate is multiplied by 2, so the order in B is **1**.
3. Rate law: **rate = k[A]²[B]** (third order overall).
4. From experiment 1:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\ce{A}]^2[\ce{B}]} \\[4pt]
   &= \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M s}^{-1}}{(0.10\ \text{M})^2(0.10\ \text{M})} \\[4pt]
   &= 2.0\ \text{M}^{-2}\ \text{s}^{-1}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The units follow from M s⁻¹ ÷ M³ = M⁻² s⁻¹.
</Example>

<Example title="Half-life">
**Question:** A first-order decomposition has $k = 6.20 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Find its half-life.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_{1/2} &= \frac{0.693}{6.20 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{s}^{-1}} \\[4pt]
&= 1.12 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is $1.12 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{min}}{60\ \text{s}} = 18.6$ min.
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Taking orders from the balanced equation">
For $\ce{2NO + O2 -> 2NO2}$ the rate law happens to be rate = k[NO]²[O₂], but that is an experimental result. Many reactions have orders that differ from their coefficients, so always use data.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Giving every k the same units">
$k$ for a first-order reaction is in s⁻¹, but for a second-order reaction it is in M⁻¹ s⁻¹. Work out the units from the rate law every time.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Thinking every half-life doubles the time to finish">
After 2 half-lives, $\frac{1}{4}$ of the reactant remains, not none. A first-order reaction never quite reaches zero.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- M/s, M s⁻¹ and mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ mean the same thing.
- "ln" is the natural logarithm (base e); ln 2 = 0.693.
- $[\ce{A}]_0$ is the initial concentration; $[\ce{A}]_t$ is the concentration at time $t$.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Rate = change in concentration ÷ time (M/s); divide by coefficients for one reaction rate.
- Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n; orders come from experiments (method of initial rates).
- Units of k: zero order M s⁻¹; first s⁻¹; second M⁻¹ s⁻¹; third M⁻² s⁻¹.
- First order: $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$, independent of starting concentration.
</RememberThis>
