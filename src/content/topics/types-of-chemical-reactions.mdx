## What is it?

Thousands of reactions fall into a few common **patterns**. Recognizing the pattern lets you predict the products and write the equation:

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis** (combination) | A + B → AB | $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$ |
| **Decomposition** | AB → A + B | $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$ |
| **Single displacement** | A + BC → AC + B | $\ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}$ |
| **Double displacement** | AB + CD → AD + CB | $\ce{AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3}$ |
| **Combustion** | fuel + O₂ → oxides | $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ |

<KeyIdea>
Look at the **reactants**: two substances joining, one substance breaking apart, an element plus a compound, two compounds swapping partners, or something burning in oxygen. The pattern tells you what the products will be; then balance the equation.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Predicting products.** You can write the equation for a reaction you have never seen.
- **Everyday chemistry.** Burning fuels (combustion), making lime from limestone (decomposition), extracting metals (displacement) and treating indigestion (neutralization, a double displacement).
- **It leads to deeper ideas.** Displacement and combustion are **redox** reactions; many double displacements are **precipitation** or **acid–base** reactions.

## How does it work?

### 1. Synthesis

Two or more substances combine into one product. Often an element + element: $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$; or a metal oxide + water: $\ce{CaO + H2O -> Ca(OH)2}$.

### 2. Decomposition

One compound breaks into simpler substances, usually when heated or by electricity:

- metal carbonates → metal oxide + carbon dioxide: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$
- electrolysis of water: $\ce{2H2O -> 2H2 + O2}$

### 3. Single displacement and the activity series

A more reactive element **displaces** a less reactive one from its compound. For metals, use the **activity series** (most reactive first):

**K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au**

- A metal displaces any metal **below** it from a solution of its salt. Zinc displaces copper:

  $$
  \ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}
  $$

  Copper does **not** displace zinc: no reaction.
- Metals above hydrogen displace hydrogen from acids: $\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$.
- Halogens: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one (F > Cl > Br > I): $\ce{Cl2 + 2KBr -> 2KCl + Br2}$.

### 4. Double displacement

Two ionic compounds **swap partners**. A reaction happens if a **precipitate** (insoluble solid), **water** or a **gas** forms:

- precipitation:

  $$
  \ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}
  $$
- neutralization: $\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}$
- gas formation:

  $$
  \ce{Na2CO3 + 2HCl -> 2NaCl + H2O + CO2}
  $$

### 5. Combustion

A substance reacts rapidly with **oxygen**, releasing heat and light. Hydrocarbons burning completely give **carbon dioxide and water**:

$$
\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}
$$

With too little oxygen, combustion is **incomplete**, producing poisonous carbon monoxide (CO) or soot (C).

<Analogy>
The patterns are like dance moves. In synthesis, two dancers pair up. In decomposition, a couple splits. In single displacement, a newcomer cuts in and takes someone's partner (but only if they are "more attractive": higher in the activity series). In double displacement, two couples swap partners.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
These categories overlap and are not the only ones. Single displacement and combustion are always **redox** reactions; many synthesis and decomposition reactions are too. Double displacement reactions are not redox: no oxidation states change. A more fundamental classification uses the **driving force**: electron transfer (redox), proton transfer (acid–base) or the formation of an insoluble solid (precipitation).
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Classifying reactions">
**Question:** Classify: (a) $\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$ (b) $\ce{Fe + CuCl2 -> FeCl2 + Cu}$ (c) $\ce{Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI -> PbI2 + 2KNO3}$ (d) $\ce{4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3}$

1. (a) One reactant → two products: **decomposition**.
2. (b) Element + compound, element swapped: **single displacement**.
3. (c) Two compounds swap partners: **double displacement** (PbI₂ precipitates).
4. (d) Two elements → one compound: **synthesis** (also a combustion of aluminium).
</Example>

<Example title="Will it react?">
**Question:** Predict whether each reaction happens: (a) $\ce{Zn + CuSO4}$ (b) $\ce{Cu + ZnSO4}$ (c) $\ce{Ag + HCl}$

1. (a) Zinc is above copper: **yes**,

   $$
   \ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}
   $$
2. (b) Copper is below zinc: **no reaction**.
3. (c) Silver is below hydrogen: **no reaction**.
</Example>

<Example title="Mass from a decomposition">
**Question:** 10.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated until it fully decomposes: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$. What mass of carbon dioxide is released?

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&10.0\ \cancel{\text{g CaCO}_3} \\[4pt]
&\quad \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{mol CaCO}_3}}{100.09\ \cancel{\text{g CaCO}_3}} \\[4pt]
&\quad \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{mol CO}_2}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol CaCO}_3}} \\[4pt]
&\quad \times \frac{44.01\ \text{g CO}_2}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol CO}_2}} \\[4pt]
&= 4.40\ \text{g CO}_2
\end{aligned}
$$
</Example>

<Example title="Writing a combustion equation">
**Question:** Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of propane, $\ce{C3H8}$.

1. Products of complete combustion: $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$.
2. Balance C (3 CO₂), then H (4 H₂O), then O: 3 × 2 + 4 × 1 = 10 O atoms, so 5 O₂.
3. Balanced equation:

   $$
   \ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}
   $$
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Predicting a displacement that can't happen">
A metal only displaces a metal **below** it in the activity series. $\ce{Cu + FeSO4}$ gives no reaction, however long you wait.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Assuming every double displacement happens">
Mixing $\ce{KNO3(aq)}$ and $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$ gives no reaction: all four possible products are soluble, so no precipitate, water or gas forms. The ions just stay in solution.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Writing diatomic elements as single atoms">
Oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen and the halogens are diatomic: $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$. "Mg + O → MgO" is not a correct equation.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.
- "Δ" or "heat" written over the arrow shows that a reaction needs heating.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Synthesis A + B → AB; decomposition AB → A + B.
- Single displacement A + BC → AC + B: only if A is more reactive (activity series).
- Double displacement AB + CD → AD + CB: only if a precipitate, water or gas forms.
- Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon gives CO₂ + H₂O.
- Classify, predict the products, then balance.
</RememberThis>
