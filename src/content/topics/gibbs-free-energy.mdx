## What is it?

Some reactions happen on their own once started (they are **spontaneous**); others need a continuous input of energy. Enthalpy alone cannot decide which: ice melts spontaneously at room temperature even though melting is **endothermic**. A second quantity is needed.

**Entropy**, $S$, measures how spread out energy and matter are: the number of ways the particles and their energy can be arranged. Gases have high entropy, liquids less, and solids the least.

The **second law of thermodynamics**: in a spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe increases.

Chemists combine enthalpy and entropy into the **Gibbs free energy** change:

$$
\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S
$$

where $T$ is the temperature in **kelvin**.

<KeyIdea>
At constant temperature and pressure, a reaction is **spontaneous** when $\Delta G$ is **negative**, at equilibrium when $\Delta G = 0$, and non-spontaneous (the reverse is spontaneous) when $\Delta G$ is positive. "Spontaneous" says nothing about speed: diamond turning into graphite is spontaneous, but immeasurably slow.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Predicting reactions.** $\Delta G$ tells chemists whether a reaction can go at all, and at what temperature it becomes possible, before they try it.
- **Industry.** Lime ($\ce{CaO}$) is made by heating limestone above about 850 °C, the temperature at which $\Delta G$ for the decomposition becomes negative.
- **Life and batteries.** Cells couple reactions with negative $\Delta G$ (such as using ATP) to drive ones with positive $\Delta G$; a battery's voltage is a direct measure of $\Delta G$ ($\Delta G = -nFE$).

## How does it work?

### 1. Predicting the sign of ΔS

Entropy **increases** ($\Delta S$ positive) when:

- a solid melts or a liquid boils (solid → liquid → gas);
- the number of **moles of gas** increases, for example $\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$;
- a solid dissolves to form ions in solution (usually);
- the temperature rises.

Entropy **decreases** when gas molecules combine into fewer molecules, for example $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$ (4 mol of gas → 2 mol).

### 2. Calculating ΔS°

Unlike enthalpies of formation, **absolute** standard entropies, $S°$, can be measured for every substance, including elements (they are not zero). Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta S° = \textstyle\sum n S°(\text{products}) \\[2pt]
&\qquad - \textstyle\sum n S°(\text{reactants})
\end{aligned}
$$

$S°$ values are in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so $\Delta S°$ comes out in J/K. **Convert it to kJ/K** before combining it with $\Delta H°$ in kJ.

### 3. The four cases

| ΔH | ΔS | ΔG = ΔH − TΔS | Spontaneous? |
|---|---|---|---|
| − | + | always negative | at all temperatures |
| + | − | always positive | never (the reverse is) |
| − | − | negative when T is low | at **low** temperature |
| + | + | negative when T is high | at **high** temperature |

In the last two cases, the reaction switches at the temperature where $\Delta G = 0$:

$$
T = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}
$$

### 4. ΔG° and the equilibrium constant

The standard free energy change is linked to the equilibrium constant:

$$
\Delta G° = -RT \ln K
$$

with $R$ = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. A large negative $\Delta G°$ means a large $K$ (products favoured); a positive $\Delta G°$ means $K$ is less than 1.

<Analogy>
Think of a tidy bedroom. Left alone, it gets messier, because there are far more messy arrangements than tidy ones: that is entropy. Tidying it needs energy (an enthalpy cost). Whether the room ends up tidy depends on both: how much energy you put in, and how strongly "messiness" wins. Temperature sets how much weight entropy gets in the balance.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
$\Delta G$ is the maximum useful (non-expansion) work a process can do at constant temperature and pressure. The relation $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ comes from the total entropy change: $\Delta S_\text{universe} = \Delta S_\text{system} - \Delta H/T$, so $\Delta G = -T\Delta S_\text{universe}$, and a negative $\Delta G$ is the same as an increase in the entropy of the universe. Using $\Delta H°$ and $\Delta S°$ at 298 K for other temperatures is an approximation, because both change a little with temperature.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="ΔS°, ΔG° and the switching temperature for ammonia synthesis">
**Question:** For $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$, $\Delta H°$ = −91.8 kJ. Using $S°$ (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹): $\ce{N2}$ 191.6, $\ce{H2}$ 130.7, $\ce{NH3}$ 192.8, find $\Delta S°$, $\Delta G°$ at 298.15 K, and the temperature above which the reaction is no longer spontaneous.

1. $\Delta S° = 2(192.8) - [191.6 + 3(130.7)] = 385.6 - 583.7 = -198.1\ \text{J/K} = -0.1981\ \text{kJ/K}$ (negative: 4 mol of gas become 2)
2. $\Delta G°$:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &\Delta G° = -91.8\ \text{kJ} \\[4pt]
   &\quad - (298.15\ \text{K}) \\[4pt]
   &\quad\quad \times (-0.1981\ \text{kJ/K}) \\[4pt]
   &\quad = -91.8\ \text{kJ} + 59.06\ \text{kJ} \\[4pt]
   &\quad = \mathbf{-32.7\ kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. Spontaneous at 298 K. Switching temperature: $T = \dfrac{\Delta H°}{\Delta S°} = \dfrac{-91.8\ \text{kJ}}{-0.1981\ \text{kJ/K}} =$ **463 K**. Above this, $\Delta G°$ is positive.
</Example>

<Example title="Why limestone must be heated">
**Question:** For $\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$, find $\Delta H°$, $\Delta S°$ and $\Delta G°$ at 298.15 K, and the minimum temperature for decomposition. (Data in the note above.)

1. $\Delta H° = [-634.9 + (-393.5)] - (-1207.6) = +179.2\ \text{kJ}$
2. $\Delta S° = [38.1 + 213.8] - 91.7 = +160.2\ \text{J/K} = +0.1602\ \text{kJ/K}$
3. $\Delta G°_{298} = 179.2\ \text{kJ} - (298.15\ \text{K})(0.1602\ \text{kJ/K}) = 179.2\ \text{kJ} - 47.76\ \text{kJ} =$ **+131.4 kJ**: not spontaneous at room temperature.
4. $T = \dfrac{179.2\ \text{kJ}}{0.1602\ \text{kJ/K}} =$ **1119 K** (about 846 °C). Lime kilns run hotter than this.
</Example>

<Example title="From ΔG° to K">
**Question:** Estimate $K$ at 298.15 K for ammonia synthesis ($\Delta G°$ = −32.7 kJ = −3.27 × 10⁴ J).

1. $\ln K = -\dfrac{\Delta G°}{RT} = -\dfrac{-3.27 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J/mol}}{(8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1})(298.15\ \text{K})} = 13.2$
2. $K = e^{13.2} \approx$ **5 × 10⁵**: products are strongly favoured at 298 K (even though the reaction is too slow without a catalyst).
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Mixing J and kJ">
$\Delta S°$ is usually in J/K but $\Delta H°$ in kJ. Convert $\Delta S°$ to kJ/K (divide by 1000) before calculating $\Delta H - T\Delta S$, or the entropy term will be 1000 times too large.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Using °C in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS">
$T$ must be in **kelvin**. At 25 °C, use 298.15 K; using 25 would make the entropy term about twelve times too small.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Thinking spontaneous means fast">
A negative $\Delta G$ says a reaction **can** happen, not how fast. Speed is kinetics (activation energy); a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen is spontaneous but does nothing until a spark starts it.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- The degree sign (°) means standard conditions: 1 bar and, unless stated, 298.15 K.
- Entropy units: J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for $S°$, J/K for $\Delta S°$ of a reaction as written.
- Some books use "feasible" or "thermodynamically favourable" instead of "spontaneous".
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Entropy: gases high, solids low; ΔS is positive when moles of gas increase.
- $\Delta S° = \sum nS°(\text{products}) - \sum nS°(\text{reactants})$, in J/K; convert to kJ/K.
- $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ (T in K). Negative ΔG: spontaneous. Switching temperature $T = \Delta H/\Delta S$.
- $\Delta G° = -RT\ln K$ and $\Delta G° = -nFE°$.
</RememberThis>
