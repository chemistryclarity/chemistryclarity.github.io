## What is it?

"Insoluble" salts are really **very slightly soluble**. When solid silver chloride is placed in water, a tiny amount dissolves until the solution is **saturated**. Then a dynamic equilibrium is set up between the solid and its ions:

$$
\ce{AgCl(s) <=> Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}
$$

The equilibrium constant is the **solubility product**, $K_\text{sp}$:

$$
K_\text{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}][\ce{Cl-}] = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}
$$

The solid does not appear in the expression (its concentration is constant). Each ion concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient: for $\ce{CaF2(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}$, $K_\text{sp} = [\ce{Ca^2+}][\ce{F-}]^2$.

<KeyIdea>
$K_\text{sp}$ is the **ceiling** for the product of the ion concentrations. If the ion product $Q$ is **below** $K_\text{sp}$, more solid can dissolve. If $Q$ is **above** $K_\text{sp}$, the solution is supersaturated and a **precipitate forms** until $Q = K_\text{sp}$.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Health.** Barium sulfate is swallowed for X-ray imaging of the gut: barium ions are toxic, but $\ce{BaSO4}$ is so insoluble ($K_\text{sp} \approx 10^{-10}$) that almost none dissolves. Kidney stones form when calcium oxalate exceeds its $K_\text{sp}$.
- **Teeth.** Fluoride converts tooth enamel into a less soluble mineral, so it dissolves less in acid.
- **Analysis and clean-up.** Chemists precipitate one ion while leaving others in solution, and remove heavy metals from waste water.

## How does it work?

### 1. Molar solubility

The **molar solubility**, $s$, is the number of moles that dissolve per litre of saturated solution. Write the ion concentrations in terms of $s$:

| Salt | Ions | $K_\text{sp}$ in terms of $s$ |
|---|---|---|
| AgCl, BaSO₄ (1 : 1) | $s$ and $s$ | $K_\text{sp} = s^2$ |
| CaF₂, PbI₂, Mg(OH)₂ (1 : 2) | $s$ and $2s$ | $K_\text{sp} = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3$ |
| Ag₂CrO₄ (2 : 1) | $2s$ and $s$ | $K_\text{sp} = 4s^3$ |

Only compare $K_\text{sp}$ values directly for salts with the **same** ion ratio; otherwise, compare their molar solubilities.

### 2. The common-ion effect

If the solution already contains one of the ions, the equilibrium shifts to the left (Le Chatelier's principle), and **less** solid dissolves. Silver chloride is far less soluble in salt water than in pure water.

### 3. Will a precipitate form?

1. Find each ion concentration **after mixing** (allow for the total volume).
2. Calculate the ion product $Q$ in the same form as $K_\text{sp}$.
3. Compare: $Q > K_\text{sp}$: precipitate forms. $Q < K_\text{sp}$: no precipitate. $Q = K_\text{sp}$: saturated.

<Analogy>
$K_\text{sp}$ is like the capacity of a crowded room. While there is space (Q below Ksp), more people can come in. Once it is full (Q = Ksp), anyone else who arrives must leave again. If you already filled the room with one group (a common ion), there is less space for anyone else.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
$K_\text{sp}$ depends on temperature. Simple $K_\text{sp}$ calculations ignore effects such as ion pairing and reactions of the ions with water: for example, the solubility of salts of weak acids (carbonates, phosphates, fluorides) **increases** in acid, because $\ce{H+}$ removes the anion. Strictly, equilibrium constants use activities rather than concentrations, which matters in concentrated solutions.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Molar solubility of AgCl">
**Question:** Calculate the molar solubility of AgCl ($K_\text{sp} = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$) in mol/L and in g/L.

1. $[\ce{Ag+}] = [\ce{Cl-}] = s$, so $K_\text{sp} = s^2$.
2. Molar solubility:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &s = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-10}} \\[4pt]
   &= 1.3 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. In g/L: $1.34 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L} \times 143.32\ \text{g/mol} = 1.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{g/L}$, about 2 mg per litre.
</Example>

<Example title="A 1 : 2 salt">
**Question:** Calculate the molar solubility of $\ce{CaF2}$ ($K_\text{sp} = 3.9 \times 10^{-11}$).

1. $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = s$ and $[\ce{F-}] = 2s$, so $K_\text{sp} = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3$.
2. Solve:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &s = \sqrt[3]{\frac{3.9 \times 10^{-11}}{4}} \\[4pt]
   &= 2.1 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
</Example>

<Example title="Ksp from solubility">
**Question:** The solubility of $\ce{PbI2}$ is 0.62 g/L. Calculate $K_\text{sp}$.

1. Molar solubility: $s = \dfrac{0.62\ \text{g/L}}{461.0\ \text{g/mol}} = 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}$
2. $[\ce{Pb^2+}] = s$, $[\ce{I-}] = 2s$:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &K_\text{sp} = 4s^3 = 4 \times (1.345 \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 10^{-3})^3 \\[4pt]
   &= 9.7 \times 10^{-9}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
</Example>

<Example title="The common-ion effect">
**Question:** Calculate the molar solubility of AgCl in 0.10 mol/L NaCl.

1. $[\ce{Cl-}] \approx 0.10$ mol/L (from the NaCl); $[\ce{Ag+}] = s$.
2. $K_\text{sp} = s \times 0.10$, so

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &s = \frac{1.8 \times 10^{-10}}{0.10} \\[4pt]
   &= 1.8 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{mol/L}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. That is about **7500 times less** than in pure water ($1.3 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L).
</Example>

<Example title="Will a precipitate form?">
**Question:** Equal volumes of 0.0010 mol/L $\ce{AgNO3}$ and 0.0010 mol/L NaCl are mixed. Does AgCl precipitate?

1. Mixing equal volumes halves each concentration: $[\ce{Ag+}] = [\ce{Cl-}] = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ mol/L.
2. Ion product:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &Q = (5.0 \times 10^{-4})(5.0 \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 10^{-4}) \\[4pt]
   &= 2.5 \times 10^{-7}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. $Q = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}$ is greater than $K_\text{sp} = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$: **a precipitate forms**.
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Forgetting to double (and square) the second ion">
For $\ce{CaF2}$, $[\ce{F-}] = 2s$, and it is squared in $K_\text{sp}$: $K_\text{sp} = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3$, not $s^2$ or $2s^2$.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Comparing Ksp values of different types of salt">
A smaller $K_\text{sp}$ does not always mean a less soluble salt. Ag₂CrO₄ ($K_\text{sp} = 1.1 \times 10^{-12}$) is actually **more** soluble than AgCl ($1.8 \times 10^{-10}$): $6.5 \times 10^{-5}$ versus $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L. Compare molar solubilities.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Ignoring dilution when solutions are mixed">
Mixing two solutions increases the volume, so every concentration falls. Recalculate the concentrations before working out $Q$.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- Solids are left out of $K_\text{sp}$ expressions; ion concentrations are in mol/L at equilibrium.
- $K_\text{sp}$ values are usually quoted without units.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- $K_\text{sp}$ = product of the ion concentrations in a saturated solution, each raised to its coefficient.
- 1 : 1 salts: $K_\text{sp} = s^2$. 1 : 2 or 2 : 1 salts: $K_\text{sp} = 4s^3$.
- A common ion lowers the solubility.
- $Q > K_\text{sp}$: precipitate. $Q < K_\text{sp}$: no precipitate. Remember to allow for dilution on mixing.
</RememberThis>
