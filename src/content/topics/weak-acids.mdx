## What is it?

A **weak acid** gives up its proton to water only **partly**. At any moment most of its molecules are still intact, and only a small fraction are ionized:

$$
\ce{HA + H2O <=> H3O+ + A-}
$$

The **acid dissociation constant**, $K_\text{a}$, measures how far this goes:

$$
K_\text{a} = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

The larger $K_\text{a}$, the stronger the acid. Chemists often use $\text{p}K_\text{a} = -\log K_\text{a}$: the **smaller** the pKa, the **stronger** the acid.

<KeyIdea>
A weak acid sets up an **equilibrium**. Because only a little of it ionizes, its pH is higher (less acidic) than a strong acid of the same concentration.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Most acids are weak.** Vinegar (acetic acid), citrus fruits (citric acid), and the acids in many medicines and foods are weak acids.
- **Biology runs on weak acids and bases.** Amino acids, the phosphate groups in DNA and the carbonic acid in blood all behave as weak acids or bases.
- **Ka is the key to buffers and titrations**, the next two lessons.

## How does it work?

### 1. Comparing acid strength

| Acid | $K_\text{a}$ (25 °C) | pKa |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrofluoric acid, $\ce{HF}$ | $6.8 \times 10^{-4}$ | 3.17 |
| Formic acid, $\ce{HCOOH}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$ | 3.74 |
| Acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | 4.74 |
| Hydrocyanic acid, $\ce{HCN}$ | $4.9 \times 10^{-10}$ | 9.31 |

HF is the strongest of these weak acids; HCN is the weakest.

### 2. Calculating the pH of a weak acid

Use an **ICE table** (Initial, Change, Equilibrium). For an acid of initial concentration $C$, let $x$ = amount that ionizes:

| | $\ce{HA}$ | $\ce{H3O+}$ | $\ce{A-}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial | $C$ | 0 | 0 |
| Change | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
| Equilibrium | $C - x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

So $K_\text{a} = \dfrac{x^2}{C - x}$. When the acid is weak, $x$ is tiny compared with $C$, so $C - x \approx C$, and:

$$
x = [\ce{H3O+}] \approx \sqrt{K_\text{a} \times C}
$$

**Check the approximation:** if $x$ is less than 5% of $C$, it is acceptable. Otherwise, solve the quadratic equation instead.

### 3. Percent ionization

$$
\%\ \text{ionized} = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}]}{C} \times 100\%
$$

### 4. Weak bases

Weak bases work the same way with $K_\text{b}$ and $\ce{OH-}$. For ammonia, $\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$, and $[\ce{OH-}] \approx \sqrt{K_\text{b} \times C}$. For a conjugate acid–base pair, $K_\text{a} \times K_\text{b} = K_\text{w}$.

<Analogy>
Imagine a dance hall where couples (HA) can split up (H⁺ and A⁻) and pair up again. With a strong acid, every couple splits and nobody pairs again. With a weak acid, couples keep splitting and re-forming, but at any moment almost all are still together. Ka tells you how many are split at any moment.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
Ka values depend on temperature (the table is for 25 °C). The "5% rule" is a convention, not a law: it keeps the error in $x$ small. A weak acid ionizes **more** (as a percentage) when it is more dilute, even though its pH rises. Strictly, equilibrium constants use activities, not concentrations, but concentrations work well for dilute solutions.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="pH of a weak acid">
**Question:** Calculate the pH and percent ionization of 0.20 M acetic acid. ($K_\text{a} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$)

1. $[\ce{H3O+}] \approx \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.20} = 1.9 \times 10^{-3}$ M
2. Check: $1.9 \times 10^{-3}$ is about 0.9% of 0.20, below 5%, so the approximation is fine.
3. pH = −log(1.9 × 10⁻³) = **2.72**; percent ionization ≈ **0.9%**

Compare: 0.20 M HCl (strong) has pH 0.70.
</Example>

<Example title="Ka from a measured pH">
**Question:** A 0.10 M solution of a weak acid has pH 3.00. Calculate $K_\text{a}$.

1. $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{A-}] = 10^{-3.00} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ M
2. $[\ce{HA}] = 0.10 - 0.0010 = 0.099$ M
3. $K_\text{a} = \dfrac{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})^2}{0.099} =$ **1.0 × 10⁻⁵**
</Example>

<Example title="pH of a weak base">
**Question:** Calculate the pH of 0.20 M ammonia. ($K_\text{b} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$)

1. $[\ce{OH-}] \approx \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.20} = 1.9 \times 10^{-3}$ M
2. pOH = 2.72, so pH = 14.00 − 2.72 = **11.28**
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Treating a weak acid like a strong acid">
For 0.20 M acetic acid, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is **not** 0.20 M. That would give pH 0.70 instead of 2.72. Only strong acids ionize completely.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing up Ka and pKa">
A **larger** Ka means a stronger acid, but a **smaller** pKa means a stronger acid. Check which one you are comparing.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Using the approximation without checking">
If $x$ is more than 5% of the starting concentration (for example with fairly strong weak acids such as HF, or very dilute solutions), the shortcut is not accurate enough, so solve the quadratic.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $K_\text{a}$ is also called the **acidity constant** or **acid ionization constant**.
- Pure liquid water is left out of the $K_\text{a}$ expression.
- Some courses write $\ce{HA <=> H+ + A-}$ instead of including water; the $K_\text{a}$ expression is the same.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Weak acids ionize only partly: $\ce{HA + H2O <=> H3O+ + A-}$.
- $K_\text{a} = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]/[\ce{HA}]$; larger Ka (smaller pKa) = stronger acid.
- Shortcut: $[\ce{H3O+}] \approx \sqrt{K_\text{a}C}$, valid if under 5% ionizes.
- Weak bases: same method with Kb and OH⁻; $K_\text{a}K_\text{b} = K_\text{w}$.
</RememberThis>
