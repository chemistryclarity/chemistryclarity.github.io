## What is it?

Acids and bases are two important families of substances, and they react with each other. Two definitions are used at this level:

| | **Acid** | **Base** |
|---|---|---|
| **Arrhenius** (in water) | Produces $\ce{H+}$ ions (more precisely $\ce{H3O+}$) when dissolved in water | Produces $\ce{OH-}$ ions when dissolved in water |
| **Brønsted–Lowry** (more general) | **Donates** a proton ($\ce{H+}$) | **Accepts** a proton ($\ce{H+}$) |

A hydrogen ion, $\ce{H+}$, is just a proton: a hydrogen atom that has lost its only electron.

<KeyIdea>
In the Brønsted–Lowry picture, an acid–base reaction is a **proton transfer**: the acid gives an $\ce{H+}$ and the base takes it.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

Acids and bases are everywhere:

- **In your body:** stomach acid contains hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl}$, which helps digest food, and the pH of blood is kept within a narrow range.
- **At home:** vinegar contains acetic acid; baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate) and many cleaning products are basic; antacid tablets neutralize excess stomach acid.
- **In the environment and industry:** soil acidity affects which crops grow; acid–base reactions are used to make fertilisers, medicines and many other products.

## How does it work?

### 1. Acids donate protons

When hydrogen chloride dissolves in water, it gives a proton to a water molecule:

$$
\ce{HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-}
$$

$\ce{HCl}$ is the acid (proton donor) and water is the base (proton acceptor). The ion $\ce{H3O+}$ is called the **hydronium ion**.

### 2. Bases accept protons

Some bases contain hydroxide ions, such as sodium hydroxide: $\ce{NaOH -> Na+ + OH-}$. The $\ce{OH-}$ ion accepts protons.

Other bases contain no $\ce{OH-}$ at all. Ammonia accepts a proton from water and *produces* $\ce{OH-}$:

$$
\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}
$$

### 3. Conjugate acid–base pairs

When an acid gives away a proton, what is left can take a proton back, so it is a base: its **conjugate base**. When a base accepts a proton it becomes its **conjugate acid**. The two members of a **conjugate pair** differ by exactly one $\ce{H+}$:

- $\ce{HCl}$ / $\ce{Cl-}$
- $\ce{H3O+}$ / $\ce{H2O}$
- $\ce{NH4+}$ / $\ce{NH3}$

### 4. Strong and weak acids and bases

- A **strong acid** ionizes **completely** in water: essentially every molecule gives up its proton. Examples: $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$ (its first proton).
- A **weak acid** ionizes only **partly**: most molecules stay intact. Example: acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$.
- **Strong bases** include the hydroxides of Group 1 metals, such as $\ce{NaOH}$ and $\ce{KOH}$. **Weak bases** include ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$.

A double arrow ($\ce{<=>}$) shows a reaction that goes only partly; a single arrow ($\ce{->}$) shows one that goes essentially to completion.

### 5. Neutralization

An acid and a base react to form a **salt** and **water**:

$$
\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}
$$

The essential change is that $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ combine to make water: $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$.

<Analogy>
Think of the proton as a ball. The acid **throws** the ball and the base **catches** it. After the throw, the thrower is empty-handed but could catch a ball back (the conjugate base), and the catcher now holds a ball it could throw again (the conjugate acid).
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
A bare $\ce{H+}$ ion doesn't float freely in water: it is always attached to water molecules. Writing $\ce{H+(aq)}$ is a convenient shorthand for $\ce{H3O+(aq)}$. There is also a broader definition, the **Lewis** definition, in which an acid accepts a pair of electrons and a base donates one. It covers reactions with no proton transfer at all.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Identifying acid, base and conjugate pairs">
**Question:** In the reaction $\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$, identify the acid, the base and the two conjugate pairs.

1. Water loses a proton (it becomes $\ce{OH-}$), so **water is the acid**.
2. Ammonia gains a proton (it becomes $\ce{NH4+}$), so **ammonia is the base**.
3. Conjugate pairs differ by one $\ce{H+}$: $\ce{NH4+}$ / $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ / $\ce{OH-}$.

**Note:** in the HCl reaction, water acted as a **base**; here it acts as an **acid**. Substances that can do both are called **amphoteric**.
</Example>

<Example title="Writing a neutralization equation">
**Question:** Write the equation for the neutralization of nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$, by potassium hydroxide, $\ce{KOH}$.

1. Acid + base → salt + water.
2. The salt combines the metal ion from the base ($\ce{K+}$) with the anion from the acid ($\ce{NO3-}$): $\ce{KNO3}$.

**Answer:** $\ce{HNO3 + KOH -> KNO3 + H2O}$
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Confusing 'strong' with 'concentrated'">
**Strong** describes how completely an acid ionizes. **Concentrated** describes how much acid is dissolved in a given volume. A dilute solution of a strong acid (HCl) and a concentrated solution of a weak acid (acetic acid) are both possible.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Thinking every base contains OH⁻">
Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, has no hydroxide ion, but it is a base because it **accepts a proton**. The Brønsted–Lowry definition covers it; the Arrhenius definition, strictly applied, does not.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing up conjugate pairs">
A conjugate pair differs by **exactly one** $\ce{H+}$. $\ce{H3O+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ are **not** a conjugate pair: they differ by two protons.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $\ce{H+(aq)}$ and $\ce{H3O+(aq)}$ are both used for the hydrated proton; many textbooks switch between them.
- "Ionize" and "dissociate" are often used interchangeably for acids in water.
- Some courses spell the term *neutralisation* (British spelling).
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Acid = proton (H⁺) **donor**; base = proton (H⁺) **acceptor**.
- Conjugate pairs differ by exactly one H⁺.
- Strong = ionizes completely; weak = ionizes partly. Strong ≠ concentrated.
- Acid + base → salt + water.
</RememberThis>
