## What is it?

**Hess's law:** the enthalpy change of a reaction is the **same whatever route** is taken from the reactants to the products. Enthalpy is a **state function**: it depends only on the start and end, not on the path.

This means you can find $\Delta H$ for a reaction that is hard to measure directly by **adding up** the $\Delta H$ values of other reactions that combine to give it.

<KeyIdea>
If equations add up to the target equation, their $\Delta H$ values add up to the target $\Delta H$. Reverse an equation: change the sign of $\Delta H$. Multiply an equation: multiply $\Delta H$ by the same number.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Impossible measurements.** The formation of CO from carbon can't be measured cleanly, because some $\ce{CO2}$ always forms. Hess's law gets it from two easy combustions.
- **Tables instead of experiments.** With a table of **enthalpies of formation**, you can calculate $\Delta H$ for thousands of reactions without doing any of them.
- **Energy planning.** Fuel values, metabolic energy and industrial heat balances all rely on these calculations.

## How does it work?

### 1. Combining equations

To reach a target equation:

1. Write the given equations so that each **target reactant** is on the left and each **target product** is on the right; **reverse** an equation if needed (and change the sign of its $\Delta H$).
2. **Multiply** equations so the amounts match the target (and multiply their $\Delta H$).
3. **Add** the equations; substances that appear on both sides cancel. Add the $\Delta H$ values.

### 2. Standard enthalpy of formation

The **standard enthalpy of formation**, $\Delta H_\text{f}°$, is the enthalpy change when **1 mol** of a compound forms from its **elements in their standard states** (the most stable form at 1 bar and 25 °C):

$$
\begin{gathered}
\ce{C(s, graphite) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)} \\[4pt]
\Delta H_\text{f}° = -393.5\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{gathered}
$$

For an element in its standard state, such as $\ce{O2(g)}$, $\ce{H2(g)}$ or C(graphite), $\Delta H_\text{f}° = 0$.

| Substance | $\Delta H_\text{f}°$ (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2(g)}$ | −393.5 |
| $\ce{CO(g)}$ | −110.5 |
| $\ce{H2O(l)}$ | −285.8 |
| $\ce{H2O(g)}$ | −241.8 |
| $\ce{CH4(g)}$ | −74.6 |
| $\ce{C2H5OH(l)}$ | −277.6 |
| $\ce{O2(g)}$, $\ce{H2(g)}$, C(graphite) | 0 |

### 3. ΔH from enthalpies of formation

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta H°_\text{rxn} = \sum n\,\Delta H_\text{f}°(\text{products}) \\[4pt]
&\qquad\quad - \sum n\,\Delta H_\text{f}°(\text{reactants})
\end{aligned}
$$

where $n$ is each coefficient (in mol) in the balanced equation. "Products minus reactants."

<Analogy>
Climbing a mountain: your change in height between the car park and the summit is the same whether you take the steep direct path or the long zig-zag trail. Enthalpy works the same way: only the start and the end matter.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
Hess's law follows from the first law of thermodynamics (energy is conserved). The formation-enthalpy formula is Hess's law applied to a two-step route: break the reactants down into their elements (the reverse of formation), then build the products from those elements. Average **bond enthalpies** give another estimate of $\Delta H$, but because they are averages, the result is only approximate.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Combining equations">
**Question:** Find $\Delta H$ for $\ce{C(s) + 1/2 O2(g) -> CO(g)}$ from:

(1) $\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}$, $\Delta H_1 = -393.5$ kJ

(2) $\ce{CO(g) + 1/2 O2(g) -> CO2(g)}$, $\Delta H_2 = -283.0$ kJ

1. Keep (1): it has C on the left, as in the target.
2. Reverse (2), so that CO is on the right: $\ce{CO2(g) -> CO(g) + 1/2 O2(g)}$, $\Delta H = +283.0$ kJ
3. Add: $\ce{CO2}$ cancels, and $\ce{O2}$ on the left becomes 1 − ½ = ½ mol:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\Delta H \\[4pt]
   &= -393.5\ \text{kJ} + 283.0\ \text{kJ} \\[4pt]
   &= -110.5\ \text{kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

This matches $\Delta H_\text{f}°$ of CO in the table.
</Example>

<Example title="Using enthalpies of formation">
**Question:** Calculate $\Delta H°$ for the combustion of methane: $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$

1. Products: $(1\ \text{mol})(-393.5\ \text{kJ/mol}) + (2\ \text{mol})(-285.8\ \text{kJ/mol}) = -965.1\ \text{kJ}$
2. Reactants: $(1\ \text{mol})(-74.6\ \text{kJ/mol}) + (2\ \text{mol})(0\ \text{kJ/mol}) = -74.6\ \text{kJ}$
3. Subtract:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\Delta H° \\[4pt]
   &= -965.1\ \text{kJ} \\[4pt]
   &\quad - (-74.6\ \text{kJ}) \\[4pt]
   &= -890.5\ \text{kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. The mol units cancel in each product, leaving kJ for the reaction as written (−890.5 kJ per mole of $\ce{CH4}$).
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Forgetting to change the sign">
When you reverse an equation, $\Delta H$ changes sign: if A → B has $\Delta H = -100$ kJ, then B → A has $\Delta H = +100$ kJ.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Forgetting the coefficients">
Multiply each $\Delta H_\text{f}°$ by its coefficient. In methane combustion there are **2 mol** of water: 2 × (−285.8 kJ/mol) = −571.6 kJ.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Reactants minus products">
The formula is **products minus reactants**. Reversing the order gives the right size with the wrong sign.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing up the states of water">
$\Delta H_\text{f}°$ of $\ce{H2O(l)}$ (−285.8 kJ/mol) and of $\ce{H2O(g)}$ (−241.8 kJ/mol) differ by 44.0 kJ/mol, the enthalpy of vaporization. Use the state in the equation.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $\Delta H_\text{f}°$ is read "standard enthalpy of formation"; the ° means standard conditions (1 bar, usually 25 °C).
- Σ ("sigma") means "the sum of".
- Equations may contain fractional coefficients (such as ½ $\ce{O2}$) when the target is "per mole" of one substance.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Hess's law: ΔH is the same by any route (enthalpy is a state function).
- Reverse an equation: change the sign of ΔH. Multiply it: multiply ΔH.
- $\Delta H_\text{f}°$ = 1 mol of compound from elements in standard states; elements have $\Delta H_\text{f}° = 0$.
- $\Delta H° = \sum n\Delta H_\text{f}°(\text{products}) - \sum n\Delta H_\text{f}°(\text{reactants})$.
</RememberThis>
