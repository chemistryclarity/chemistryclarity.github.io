## What is it?

**Energy** is the capacity to do work or to transfer heat. It comes in two broad kinds:

- **Kinetic energy**, the energy of motion: $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. The random motion of particles is **thermal energy**.
- **Potential energy**, energy stored because of position or arrangement: a raised object ($E_\text{p} = mgh$), charges near each other, or the **chemical energy** stored in the arrangement of atoms and electrons in bonds.

Energy is measured in **joules** (J). One joule is the energy of a force of one newton acting over one metre: $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2}$. Other units you will meet:

| Unit | In joules |
|---|---|
| kilojoule, kJ | $10^{3}$ J |
| calorie, cal | 4.184 J (exactly) |
| food Calorie, Cal (= 1 kcal) | 4184 J |
| kilowatt-hour, kWh | $3.6 \times 10^{6}$ J |
| litre-atmosphere, L·atm | 101.325 J |

Energy moves between things in only two ways: as **heat** ($q$), a transfer caused by a **temperature difference**, or as **work** ($w$), a transfer caused by a **force acting through a distance**, such as a gas pushing back a piston.

<KeyIdea>
Energy is **never created or destroyed**, only transferred or changed from one form to another. This is the **first law of thermodynamics**. For a chemical system:

$$
\Delta U = q + w
$$

The change in the system's **internal energy**, $\Delta U$, equals the heat added to it plus the work done on it.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Every reaction involves energy.** Burning fuel, charging a battery and digesting food all convert chemical energy into heat, work or electrical energy.
- **It is the foundation of thermochemistry.** Enthalpy, Hess's law and Gibbs free energy are all built on the first law.
- **Everyday units.** Food labels give energy in kJ and kcal; electricity bills in kWh. Converting between them is the same dimensional analysis you already know.

## How does it work?

### 1. System and surroundings

The **system** is the part we study (for example, the chemicals in a flask); the **surroundings** are everything else. Systems can be:

- **open**: matter and energy can cross the boundary (an open pan of boiling water);
- **closed**: energy can cross but matter cannot (a sealed flask);
- **isolated**: neither can cross (an ideal sealed, insulated flask).

### 2. Heat is not temperature

**Temperature** measures how hot something is: it reflects the **average** kinetic energy of its particles. **Heat** is **energy in transit** from a hotter object to a colder one. A bath of warm water has a lower temperature than a cup of boiling water, but it holds far more thermal energy, because it contains far more particles. Heat always flows from **higher to lower temperature**, until both are at the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).

### 3. Work done by a gas

When a gas expands against a constant external pressure, it pushes the surroundings back and does work on them:

$$
w = -P_\text{ext}\,\Delta V
$$

The minus sign means that when the gas **expands** ($\Delta V$ positive) the system **loses** energy ($w$ negative). When the gas is **compressed**, $w$ is positive. If the volume does not change, no expansion work is done ($w = 0$).

### 4. Sign convention

Chemistry looks at everything **from the system's point of view**:

| | Positive (+) | Negative (−) |
|---|---|---|
| $q$ | Heat flows **into** the system | Heat flows **out** |
| $w$ | Work is done **on** the system | Work is done **by** the system |
| $\Delta U$ | Internal energy **increases** | Internal energy **decreases** |

<Analogy>
Think of the system's internal energy as a bank account. Heat and work are the only two kinds of transaction: deposits are positive, withdrawals are negative. The balance ($U$) only changes by the sum of the transactions, and money is never printed or destroyed.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
Internal energy is a **state function**: $\Delta U$ depends only on the start and end states, not on the route. Heat and work are **not** state functions: the same $\Delta U$ can be reached with different splits of $q$ and $w$. Many physics books write the first law as $\Delta U = Q - W$, where $W$ is work done **by** the system; it is the same law with a different sign convention. At constant pressure, the heat transferred equals the **enthalpy change**, $q_P = \Delta H$, which is why chemists usually measure $\Delta H$ (see [Enthalpy and Calorimetry](/chemistry/enthalpy/)).
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Kinetic energy">
**Question:** A 0.145 kg baseball travels at 40.0 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \\[4pt]
&= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.145\ \text{kg} \times (40.0\ \text{m/s})^2 \\[4pt]
&= 116\ \text{kg m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2} \\[4pt]
&= 116\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$
</Example>

<Example title="Converting energy units">
**Question:** A snack bar provides 250 kcal. Express this in kilojoules.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&250\ \cancel{\text{kcal}} \times \frac{4.184\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \cancel{\text{kcal}}} \\[4pt]
&= 1046\ \text{kJ} = 1.05 \times 10^{3}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$
</Example>

<Example title="Work done by an expanding gas">
**Question:** A gas expands from 2.00 L to 5.00 L against a constant external pressure of 1.50 atm. Calculate the work in joules.

1. Change in volume: $\Delta V = 5.00\ \text{L} - 2.00\ \text{L} = 3.00\ \text{L}$
2. Work:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &w = -P_\text{ext}\,\Delta V \\[4pt]
   &= -1.50\ \text{atm} \times 3.00\ \text{L} \\[4pt]
   &= -4.50\ \cancel{\text{L atm}} \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times \frac{101.325\ \text{J}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L atm}}} \\[4pt]
   &= -456\ \text{J}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. The sign is negative: the gas does work **on the surroundings** and loses 456 J.
</Example>

<Example title="The first law">
**Question:** While expanding as in the last example, the gas absorbs 1.20 kJ of heat. What is $\Delta U$?

1. Heat flows in, so $q = +1.20\ \text{kJ} = +1200\ \text{J}$. From the last example, $w = -456\ \text{J}$.
2. First law:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\Delta U = q + w \\[4pt]
   &= (+1200\ \text{J}) + (-456\ \text{J}) \\[4pt]
   &= +744\ \text{J}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. The internal energy of the gas **increases** by 744 J: it gained more energy as heat than it lost as work.
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Treating heat and temperature as the same thing">
Temperature is a property of an object (in °C or K); heat is energy transferred (in J). A spark at 1000 °C carries very little heat; a warm bath at 40 °C can deliver a lot.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Getting the sign of work wrong">
An **expanding** gas does work on the surroundings, so $w$ is **negative**. Check: the system is spending energy, so its share must go down.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Leaving work in L·atm">
$P\Delta V$ in atm × L gives L·atm, not joules. Multiply by 101.325 J/(L·atm) before adding $w$ to a heat in joules.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $U$ is internal energy (some books use $E$); $q$ is heat; $w$ is work.
- The symbol Δ ("change in") always means final minus initial: $\Delta V = V_\text{final} - V_\text{initial}$.
- A food "Calorie" with a capital C is a kilocalorie: 1 Cal = 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Energy: kinetic ($\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$) or potential ($mgh$, chemical); unit J; 1 cal = 4.184 J; 1 L·atm = 101.325 J.
- Energy crosses a boundary only as heat ($q$, driven by a temperature difference) or work ($w$, a force through a distance).
- Temperature is not heat: temperature measures average particle energy; heat is energy in transit.
- Gas expansion work: $w = -P_\text{ext}\Delta V$; no volume change means no expansion work.
- First law: $\Delta U = q + w$; positive means energy **into** the system.
</RememberThis>
