## What is it?

Standard cell potentials, $E°$, apply only when every dissolved species is at **1 mol/L** (and gases at 1 bar). Real cells are rarely standard, and as a battery runs, the concentrations change. The **Nernst equation** gives the actual cell potential $E$:

$$
E = E° - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q
$$

where $n$ is the number of electrons transferred, $F = 96\,485$ C/mol is the Faraday constant and $Q$ is the reaction quotient. At 25 °C this becomes:

$$
E = E° - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log Q
$$

<KeyIdea>
As a cell runs, products build up and reactants are used, so **Q increases** and **E falls**. When $Q$ reaches $K$, the reaction is at equilibrium and $E = 0$: the battery is **flat**.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Batteries.** The Nernst equation explains why a battery's voltage drops as it is used.
- **Measuring concentrations.** pH meters and ion-selective electrodes measure a voltage that depends on concentration through the Nernst equation.
- **Biology.** Nerve cells hold different ion concentrations inside and outside; the resulting voltage (membrane potential) is a concentration cell.

## How does it work?

### 1. Using the Nernst equation

1. Write the balanced overall equation and find $E°_\text{cell}$ and $n$.
2. Write $Q$ (products over reactants; leave out solids).
3. Substitute into $E = E° - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log Q$.

### 2. Concentration cells

Two half-cells of the **same** metal in solutions of **different** concentration still give a voltage, even though $E° = 0$. Electrons flow so as to make the concentrations equal: the dilute side is oxidized (anode) and the concentrated side is reduced (cathode).

### 3. E°, ΔG° and K

Three quantities describe how far a reaction "wants" to go, and each can be found from the others:

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&\Delta G° = -nFE° \\[4pt]
&\Delta G° = -RT\ln K \\[4pt]
&\log K \\[4pt]
&= \frac{nE°}{0.0592\ \text{V}}\ \ \text{(at 25 °C)}
\end{aligned}
$$

| $E°_\text{cell}$ | $\Delta G°$ | $K$ | Reaction at standard conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| positive | negative | greater than 1 | spontaneous, products favoured |
| zero | zero | 1 | at equilibrium |
| negative | positive | less than 1 | non-spontaneous, reactants favoured |

<Analogy>
A cell is like water flowing between two tanks. $E°$ is the height difference when both tanks start at a standard level. As water flows, the upper tank empties and the lower one fills, so the height difference (E) shrinks until the levels are equal and the flow stops (equilibrium, E = 0).
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
The value 0.0592 V comes from $\frac{RT}{F}\ln 10$ at 298.15 K; at other temperatures use the full form. Because $\log K = nE°/0.0592$, a standard potential of only about 0.2 V for a 2-electron reaction already gives $K \approx 10^{7}$: small voltages correspond to very large equilibrium constants. Strictly, $Q$ uses activities, so the Nernst equation is most accurate for dilute solutions.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="A non-standard Zn–Cu cell">
**Question:** Calculate $E$ at 25 °C for

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

when $[\ce{Zn^2+}] = 1.0$ mol/L and $[\ce{Cu^2+}] = 0.010$ mol/L.

1. $E° = 0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.76\ \text{V}) = 1.10\ \text{V}$; $n = 2$.
2. $Q = \dfrac{[\ce{Zn^2+}]}{[\ce{Cu^2+}]} = \dfrac{1.0}{0.010} = 100$
3. Nernst:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &E = 1.10\ \text{V} \\[4pt]
   &\quad - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{2}\log 100 \\[4pt]
   &= 1.10\ \text{V} - 0.0592\ \text{V} \\[4pt]
   &= 1.04\ \text{V}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. Less $\ce{Cu^2+}$ (a reactant) means a slightly lower voltage.
</Example>

<Example title="A concentration cell">
**Question:** Two copper electrodes dip into 0.0010 mol/L and 1.0 mol/L $\ce{Cu^2+}$. Calculate the cell potential at 25 °C.

1. $E° = 0$ V (same half-reaction); $n = 2$. The dilute side is the anode: $Q = \dfrac{[\ce{Cu^2+}]_\text{dilute}}{[\ce{Cu^2+}]_\text{concentrated}} = \dfrac{0.0010}{1.0}$.
2. Nernst:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &E = 0 - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{2}\log(1.0 \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 10^{-3}) \\[4pt]
   &= 0.089\ \text{V}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
</Example>

<Example title="From E° to K">
**Question:** Calculate $K$ at 25 °C for the Zn–Cu reaction ($E° = 1.10$ V, $n = 2$).

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&\log K = \frac{2 \times 1.10\ \text{V}}{0.0592\ \text{V}} \\[4pt]
&= 37.16 \\[4pt]
&K = 10^{37.16} = 1.5 \times 10^{37}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reaction goes essentially to completion.
</Example>

<Example title="ΔG from E">
**Question:** Calculate $\Delta G$ for the non-standard Zn–Cu cell above ($E = 1.04$ V).

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&\Delta G = -nFE \\[4pt]
&= -2 \times 96\,485\ \tfrac{\text{C}}{\text{mol}} \times 1.04\ \text{V} \\[4pt]
&= -2.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/mol} \\[4pt]
&= -201\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

(1 C × 1 V = 1 J.)
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Using the wrong n">
$n$ is the number of electrons transferred in the **balanced overall** equation: 2 for Zn + Cu²⁺, not 1.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Getting Q upside down">
$Q$ is products over reactants, like $K$. For $\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}$, $Q = [\ce{Zn^2+}]/[\ce{Cu^2+}]$. Solids (Zn, Cu) are left out.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing ln and log">
$\frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$ and $\frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log Q$ are the same at 25 °C. Don't use 0.0592 with ln.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $E°$: standard potential; $E$: actual potential. $F$ = 96 485 C/mol.
- 1 V = 1 J/C, so $nFE$ comes out in J/mol.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Nernst: $E = E° - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$; at 25 °C, $E = E° - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log Q$.
- As the cell runs, Q rises and E falls; at equilibrium Q = K and E = 0.
- Concentration cells: same electrodes, different concentrations, E° = 0 but E ≠ 0.
- $\Delta G° = -nFE° = -RT\ln K$; $\log K = nE°/0.0592$ V at 25 °C.
</RememberThis>
