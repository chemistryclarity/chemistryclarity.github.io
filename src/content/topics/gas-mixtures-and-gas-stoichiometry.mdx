## What is it?

In a mixture of gases that don't react, each gas behaves as if it were alone in the container. The pressure each gas would exert on its own is its **partial pressure**. **Dalton's law** says the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures:

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&P_\text{total} \\[4pt]
&= P_\text{A} + P_\text{B} + P_\text{C} + \cdots
\end{aligned}
$$

Because pressure depends only on the number of moles (at fixed $V$ and $T$), each gas's share of the pressure equals its share of the moles, its **mole fraction** $x$:

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&P_\text{A} = x_\text{A} P_\text{total} \\[4pt]
&x_\text{A} = \frac{n_\text{A}}{n_\text{total}}
\end{aligned}
$$

<KeyIdea>
For gases, **volume is a measure of moles**. At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of moles (Avogadro's law). So $PV = nRT$ turns any gas volume into moles, and moles are the link to the balanced equation.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Breathing and diving.** Air is about 21 % oxygen, so the partial pressure of oxygen at sea level is about 0.21 atm. At high altitude it is lower, which is why climbers carry oxygen; divers breathing compressed air risk taking in too much nitrogen.
- **Collecting gases in the lab.** Gases collected over water are mixed with water vapour, which has to be subtracted.
- **Airbags, baking and industry.** Calculating the volume of gas a reaction produces is gas stoichiometry.

## How does it work?

### 1. Partial pressures

Find each partial pressure with $P_\text{A} = n_\text{A}RT/V$, or from the mole fraction. Add them to get the total.

### 2. Collecting a gas over water

A gas bubbled into an upturned bottle of water becomes saturated with water vapour. The pressure inside equals atmospheric pressure, so:

$$
P_\text{gas} = P_\text{total} - P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

The vapour pressure of water depends only on temperature (23.8 mmHg at 25 °C).

### 3. Molar volume

One mole of any ideal gas occupies **22.4 L at 0 °C and 1 atm**, and **24.5 L at 25 °C and 1 atm**. (Check which "standard" conditions your course uses; IUPAC STP, 0 °C and 1 bar, gives 22.7 L/mol.)

### 4. Gas stoichiometry

1. Convert what you know to **moles** (from mass, or from a gas volume with $n = PV/RT$).
2. Use the **mole ratio** from the balanced equation.
3. Convert to what you want (mass, or a gas volume with $V = nRT/P$).

<Analogy>
Partial pressures are like voices in a choir. Each singer adds to the total sound, and a section with more singers is louder. The total volume of sound is the sum of every section, and each section's share depends only on how many singers it has.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
Dalton's law holds exactly for ideal gases and very closely for real gases at ordinary pressures. Because the partial pressure is proportional to moles, mole fraction is also the **volume fraction** of an ideal-gas mixture: air is about 78 % N₂, 21 % O₂ and 1 % Ar by volume and by moles. Vapour pressure tables give the partial pressure of water above liquid water at each temperature; at 100 °C it reaches 760 mmHg, which is why water boils there at 1 atm.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Partial pressures">
**Question:** A 5.00 L flask at 25 °C contains 0.200 mol of N₂ and 0.0500 mol of O₂. Find each partial pressure and the total pressure.

1. $T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15$ K. It saves work to find $RT$ first:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &RT = 0.08206\ \tfrac{\text{L atm}}{\text{mol K}} \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 298.15\ \text{K} \\[4pt]
   &= 24.47\ \text{L atm/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. Nitrogen:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &nRT = 0.200\ \text{mol} \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 24.47\ \tfrac{\text{L atm}}{\text{mol}} \\[4pt]
   &= 4.894\ \text{L atm} \\[4pt]
   &P_{\text{N}_2} = \frac{nRT}{V} \\[4pt]
   &= \frac{4.894\ \text{L atm}}{5.00\ \text{L}} \\[4pt]
   &= 0.979\ \text{atm}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. Oxygen has a quarter of the moles, so $P_{\text{O}_2} = 0.245$ atm.
4. Total: $P = 0.979\ \text{atm} + 0.245\ \text{atm} = 1.22\ \text{atm}$
</Example>

<Example title="A gas collected over water">
**Question:** Hydrogen is collected over water at 25 °C. The volume is 0.250 L and the total pressure is 755 mmHg. How many moles of H₂ were collected?

1. Pressure of the dry hydrogen:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &P_{\text{H}_2} = 755\ \text{mmHg} \\[4pt]
   &\quad - 23.8\ \text{mmHg} \\[4pt]
   &= 731.2\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}} \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times \frac{1\ \text{atm}}{760\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}}} \\[4pt]
   &= 0.9621\ \text{atm}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. Moles, using $RT = 24.47$ L atm/mol at 25 °C (from the first example):

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &n = \frac{PV}{RT} \\[4pt]
   &= \frac{0.9621\ \text{atm} \times 0.250\ \text{L}}{24.47\ \text{L atm/mol}} \\[4pt]
   &= 9.83 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
</Example>

<Example title="Volume of gas from a reaction">
**Question:** What volume of CO₂, at 25 °C and 1.00 atm, is released when 10.0 g of CaCO₃ decomposes? $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$

1. Moles of CaCO₃: $\dfrac{10.0\ \text{g}}{100.09\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.09991\ \text{mol}$, so 0.09991 mol CO₂ (1 : 1).
2. Volume, with $RT = 24.47$ L atm/mol at 25 °C:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &nRT = 0.09991\ \text{mol} \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 24.47\ \tfrac{\text{L atm}}{\text{mol}} \\[4pt]
   &= 2.4448\ \text{L atm} \\[4pt]
   &V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{2.4448\ \text{L atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} \\[4pt]
   &= 2.44\ \text{L}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
</Example>

<Example title="Using molar volume">
**Question:** 5.00 g of potassium chlorate is heated: $\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$. What volume of O₂ forms at 0 °C and 1 atm?

$$
\small\begin{aligned}
&5.00\ \cancel{\text{g KClO}_3} \\[4pt]
&\times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{mol KClO}_3}}{122.55\ \cancel{\text{g KClO}_3}} \\[4pt]
&\quad \times \frac{3\ \cancel{\text{mol O}_2}}{2\ \cancel{\text{mol KClO}_3}} \\[4pt]
&\quad \times \frac{22.4\ \text{L O}_2}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol O}_2}} \\[4pt]
&= 1.37\ \text{L O}_2
\end{aligned}
$$
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Forgetting the water vapour">
A gas collected over water is not pure. Subtract the vapour pressure of water before using $PV = nRT$, or the moles of gas will be too high.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Using 22.4 L/mol at room temperature">
22.4 L/mol applies only at **0 °C and 1 atm**. At 25 °C and 1 atm, a mole of gas occupies 24.5 L. When conditions are not standard, use $PV = nRT$.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing pressure units">
With $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K), pressure must be in atm. Convert mmHg (÷ 760) or kPa (÷ 101.325) first.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $x_\text{A}$ (or $\chi_\text{A}$) is the mole fraction; mole fractions of all components add up to 1.
- 1 mmHg = 1 torr.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Dalton: $P_\text{total} = P_\text{A} + P_\text{B} + \cdots$; $P_\text{A} = x_\text{A}P_\text{total}$.
- Over water: $P_\text{gas} = P_\text{total} - P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$.
- Molar volume: 22.4 L/mol at 0 °C, 1 atm; 24.5 L/mol at 25 °C, 1 atm.
- Gas stoichiometry: gas volume ↔ moles with $PV = nRT$; moles ↔ moles with the equation.
</RememberThis>
