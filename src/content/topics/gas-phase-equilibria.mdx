## What is it?

For reactions of gases, it is often easier to measure **partial pressures** than concentrations. The equilibrium constant written with partial pressures is $K_\text{p}$. For

$$
\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}
$$

$$
K_\text{p} = \frac{(P_{\ce{NH3}})^2}{P_{\ce{N2}}\,(P_{\ce{H2}})^3}
$$

It has exactly the same form as $K_\text{c}$, with each partial pressure (in atm) in place of a concentration.

A **heterogeneous** equilibrium involves more than one phase. **Pure solids and pure liquids are left out** of $K$, because their "concentration" does not change as they are used up. For the decomposition of limestone:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)} \\[4pt]
&K_\text{p} = P_{\ce{CO2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

<KeyIdea>
$K_\text{c}$ and $K_\text{p}$ describe the same equilibrium in different units. Because $P = \frac{n}{V}RT = cRT$ for each gas, they are linked by

$$
K_\text{p} = K_\text{c}\,(RT)^{\Delta n}
$$

where $\Delta n$ = moles of gaseous products − moles of gaseous reactants. When $\Delta n = 0$, $K_\text{p} = K_\text{c}$.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Industry.** Ammonia, sulfuric acid and methanol are made by gas-phase equilibria; engineers use $K_\text{p}$ to choose pressures and temperatures.
- **The atmosphere.** Reactions such as $\ce{N2O4 <=> 2NO2}$ (a brown gas in smog) are gas equilibria.
- **Solids and gases together.** Limestone kilns, rusting and the equilibrium vapour pressure of water are all heterogeneous equilibria.

## How does it work?

### 1. Writing Kp

Products over reactants, each partial pressure raised to its coefficient; leave out solids and liquids.

### 2. Converting between Kc and Kp

1. Find $\Delta n$ from the **gas** coefficients only.
2. Use $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K) and $T$ in kelvin.
3. $K_\text{p} = K_\text{c}(RT)^{\Delta n}$.

### 3. ICE tables with pressures

Pressures behave like concentrations: set up Initial, Change and Equilibrium rows in **atm**, substitute into $K_\text{p}$ and solve.

<Analogy>
Kc and Kp are like the same price written in two currencies. The exchange rate is $(RT)^{\Delta n}$. If the reaction has the same number of gas molecules on both sides, there is nothing to convert: the price is identical in both.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
Strictly, equilibrium constants use dimensionless activities: each partial pressure is divided by the standard pressure (1 bar, or 1 atm in older tables), which is why $K$ has no units. Values of $K_\text{p}$ therefore depend slightly on whether pressures are in bar or atm. The $K$ in $\Delta G° = -RT\ln K$ is $K_\text{p}$ for gas reactions.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Kp from partial pressures">
**Question:** At 25 °C, an equilibrium mixture contains $\ce{N2O4}$ at 0.70 atm and $\ce{NO2}$ at 0.32 atm. Calculate $K_\text{p}$ for $\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&K_\text{p} = \frac{(P_{\ce{NO2}})^2}{P_{\ce{N2O4}}} = \frac{(0.32)^2}{0.70} \\[4pt]
&= 0.15
\end{aligned}
$$
</Example>

<Example title="From Kc to Kp">
**Question:** For $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$, $K_\text{c} = 0.50$ at 400 °C. Find $K_\text{p}$.

1. $\Delta n = 2 - (1 + 3) = -2$; $T = 400 + 273.15 = 673.15$ K.
2. $RT$:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &RT = 0.08206\ \tfrac{\text{L atm}}{\text{mol K}} \\[4pt]
   &\quad \times 673.15\ \text{K} \\[4pt]
   &= 55.24\ \text{L atm/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. Then, using the number 55.24 (K values are written without units):

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &K_\text{p} = 0.50 \times (55.24)^{-2} \\[4pt]
   &= 1.6 \times 10^{-4}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
</Example>

<Example title="A heterogeneous equilibrium">
**Question:** Write $K_\text{c}$ and $K_\text{p}$ for $\ce{C(s) + H2O(g) <=> CO(g) + H2(g)}$.

Carbon is a pure solid, so it is left out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&K_\text{c} = \frac{[\ce{CO}][\ce{H2}]}{[\ce{H2O}]} \\[4pt]
&K_\text{p} = \frac{P_{\ce{CO}}\,P_{\ce{H2}}}{P_{\ce{H2O}}}
\end{aligned}
$$
</Example>

<Example title="An ICE table with pressures">
**Question:** $\ce{PCl5(g) <=> PCl3(g) + Cl2(g)}$ has $K_\text{p} = 1.05$ at a certain temperature. Pure $\ce{PCl5}$ at 1.00 atm is allowed to reach equilibrium. Find the equilibrium partial pressures.

1. ICE (atm): $\ce{PCl5}$: $1.00 - x$; $\ce{PCl3}$: $x$; $\ce{Cl2}$: $x$.
2. Substitute:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\frac{x^2}{1.00 - x} = 1.05 \\[4pt]
   &x^2 + 1.05x - 1.05 = 0
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. Quadratic formula, positive root, with $a = 1$, $b = 1.05$, $c = -1.05$:

   $$
   \small\begin{aligned}
   &x = \frac{-b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \\[4pt]
   &= \frac{-1.05 + \sqrt{5.3025}}{2} \\[4pt]
   &= 0.626
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. $P_{\ce{PCl3}} = P_{\ce{Cl2}} = 0.626$ atm and $P_{\ce{PCl5}} = 1.00 - 0.626 = 0.374$ atm. Check: $\dfrac{0.626^2}{0.374} = 1.05$ ✓.
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Including solids or liquids">
In $\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$, $K_\text{p} = P_{\ce{CO2}}$ only. Writing $\dfrac{P_{\ce{CaO}}P_{\ce{CO2}}}{P_{\ce{CaCO3}}}$ is wrong: solids have no partial pressure.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Counting all moles in Δn">
Δn counts **gas** moles only. For $\ce{C(s) + H2O(g) <=> CO(g) + H2(g)}$, $\Delta n = 2 - 1 = +1$, not $2 - 2$.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Using the wrong R or temperature">
With pressures in atm, use $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K), not 8.314, and always convert °C to K.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $P_{\ce{A}}$ means the partial pressure of A at equilibrium (in atm here).
- Kc uses mol/L; Kp uses partial pressures. Both are quoted without units.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- $K_\text{p}$: same form as $K_\text{c}$, with partial pressures.
- $K_\text{p} = K_\text{c}(RT)^{\Delta n}$; Δn = gas moles (products − reactants); R = 0.08206; T in K.
- Leave pure solids and liquids out of K.
- ICE tables work with pressures just as with concentrations.
</RememberThis>
