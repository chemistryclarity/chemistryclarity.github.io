## What is it?

Every compound has a **formula** and a **name**. The formula shows which elements are present and in what ratio; the name says the same thing in words. $\ce{NaCl}$ is sodium chloride; $\ce{CO2}$ is carbon dioxide. Chemists around the world follow the same naming rules, set by IUPAC (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry), so a name means exactly one substance.

There are two main families, named in different ways:

- **Ionic compounds**, made of a **metal** (or the ammonium ion) combined with a non-metal: name the ions. Example: $\ce{MgCl2}$, magnesium chloride.
- **Molecular (covalent) compounds**, made of **non-metals only**: use Greek prefixes to count the atoms. Example: $\ce{N2O4}$, dinitrogen tetroxide.

<KeyIdea>
An ionic compound has **no overall charge**: the positive charges of the cations exactly cancel the negative charges of the anions. That single rule lets you write the formula of any ionic compound from its name.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **It's the language of chemistry.** Equations, labels, recipes and safety data sheets all use formulas and names.
- **Safety.** Sodium chloride is table salt; sodium chlorate is a weedkiller and strong oxidizer. One syllable separates them.
- **Calculations depend on it.** You can't find a molar mass or balance an equation without the correct formula.

## How does it work?

### 1. Common ions

You will meet these ions again and again:

| Positive ions (cations) | Negative ions (anions) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{H+}$ hydrogen, $\ce{Na+}$ sodium, $\ce{K+}$ potassium, $\ce{Ag+}$ silver | $\ce{F-}$ fluoride, $\ce{Cl-}$ chloride, $\ce{Br-}$ bromide, $\ce{I-}$ iodide |
| $\ce{NH4+}$ ammonium | $\ce{OH-}$ hydroxide, $\ce{NO3-}$ nitrate, $\ce{HCO3-}$ hydrogencarbonate |
| $\ce{Mg^2+}$ magnesium, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ calcium, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ barium, $\ce{Zn^2+}$ zinc | $\ce{O^2-}$ oxide, $\ce{S^2-}$ sulfide |
| $\ce{Al^3+}$ aluminium | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ sulfate, $\ce{CO3^2-}$ carbonate |
| $\ce{Fe^2+}$ iron(II), $\ce{Fe^3+}$ iron(III) | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ phosphate, $\ce{N^3-}$ nitride |
| $\ce{Cu+}$ copper(I), $\ce{Cu^2+}$ copper(II) | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ ethanoate (acetate) |

Groups of atoms that carry a charge together, such as $\ce{SO4^2-}$, are **polyatomic ions**.

### 2. Naming ionic compounds

- Name the **cation first**, then the **anion**: $\ce{KBr}$ is potassium bromide.
- A simple anion from one element ends in **-ide**: chloride, oxide, sulfide.
- Most polyatomic anions containing oxygen end in **-ate**: sulfate, nitrate, carbonate.
- A metal that can form more than one ion (many transition metals) gets a **Roman numeral** for its charge: $\ce{FeCl2}$ is iron(II) chloride; $\ce{FeCl3}$ is iron(III) chloride.
- **No prefixes** are used: $\ce{CaCl2}$ is calcium chloride, not calcium dichloride.

### 3. Writing ionic formulas

1. Write the two ions with their charges.
2. Choose the **smallest numbers** of each ion that make the total charge zero.
3. Write the numbers as subscripts (leave out 1). Put a **polyatomic ion in brackets** if you need more than one of it: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$.

### 4. Naming molecular compounds

Use a Greek prefix for the number of each kind of atom, and end the second element with **-ide**:

| Number | Prefix | Number | Prefix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | mono | 6 | hexa |
| 2 | di | 7 | hepta |
| 3 | tri | 8 | octa |
| 4 | tetra | 9 | nona |
| 5 | penta | 10 | deca |

- "Mono" is **not** used for the first element: $\ce{CO}$ is carbon monoxide, not monocarbon monoxide.
- The final "a" or "o" of the prefix is dropped before "oxide": monoxide, tetroxide, pentoxide.

Examples: $\ce{CO2}$ carbon dioxide, $\ce{SO3}$ sulfur trioxide, $\ce{N2O5}$ dinitrogen pentoxide, $\ce{SF6}$ sulfur hexafluoride. Some molecules keep common names: $\ce{H2O}$ is water, $\ce{NH3}$ is ammonia and $\ce{CH4}$ is methane.

### 5. Common acids

Acids are named by tradition: $\ce{HCl}$ hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$ nitric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$ sulfuric acid, $\ce{H3PO4}$ phosphoric acid, $\ce{H2CO3}$ carbonic acid and $\ce{CH3COOH}$ ethanoic (acetic) acid.

<Analogy>
Think of charges as weights on a see-saw that must balance exactly. To balance $\ce{Al^3+}$ (3 units on one side) against $\ce{O^2-}$ (2 units on the other), the smallest balanced load is two aluminium ions (6 units) against three oxide ions (6 units). So aluminium oxide is $\ce{Al2O3}$.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
The Roman numeral is the **oxidation state** of the metal (see [Redox Reactions](/chemistry/redox-reactions/)). Older names used endings instead, such as ferrous ($\ce{Fe^2+}$) and ferric ($\ce{Fe^3+}$); you may still see them on old bottles. **Hydrates** include water in the crystal: $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ is copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate. IUPAC prefers "hydrogencarbonate" to the older "bicarbonate".
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Formula from a name: aluminium oxide">
**Question:** Write the formula of aluminium oxide.

1. Ions: $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$.
2. The smallest whole-number total that both 3 and 2 divide into is 6, so use 2 aluminium ions and 3 oxide ions:

   $$
   2 \times (+3) + 3 \times (-2) = 0
   $$

3. Formula: $\ce{Al2O3}$.
</Example>

<Example title="A polyatomic ion: calcium nitrate">
**Question:** Write the formula of calcium nitrate.

1. Ions: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$. Two nitrate ions balance one calcium ion: $(+2) + 2 \times (-1) = 0$.
2. Two nitrate ions need brackets: $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$. The subscript 2 applies to the whole $\ce{NO3}$ group, so the formula contains 1 Ca, 2 N and 6 O.
</Example>

<Example title="Name from a formula: FeCl₃">
**Question:** Name $\ce{FeCl3}$.

1. Iron forms more than one ion, so find its charge. Three chloride ions give $3 \times (-1) = -3$.
2. The compound is neutral, so iron is $+3$: **iron(III) chloride**.
</Example>

<Example title="Naming a molecular compound">
**Question:** Name $\ce{P4O10}$ and write the formula of nitrogen trifluoride.

1. Two non-metals, so use prefixes: 4 = tetra, 10 = deca. $\ce{P4O10}$ is **tetraphosphorus decoxide**.
2. Nitrogen trifluoride: no prefix on nitrogen means 1, tri means 3: $\ce{NF3}$.
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Using prefixes for ionic compounds">
$\ce{MgCl2}$ is magnesium chloride, **not** magnesium dichloride. The charges already fix the ratio, so ionic names never use prefixes. Prefixes are only for compounds of non-metals.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing up -ide and -ate">
Sodium sulfide is $\ce{Na2S}$; sodium sulfate is $\ce{Na2SO4}$. "-ide" usually means a single element; "-ate" means a polyatomic ion with oxygen.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Forgetting brackets, or writing charges in the formula">
Calcium hydroxide is $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, not $\ce{CaOH2}$, which would mean one O and two H. And the charges are used to work out the formula but are **not written** in it: $\ce{NaCl}$, not $\ce{Na+Cl-}$.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- The Roman numeral follows the metal name directly, with no space: iron(III) chloride.
- A charge is written as a superscript with the number first, then the sign: $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- Metal (or $\ce{NH4+}$) + non-metal: **ionic**. Name the cation, then the anion; no prefixes.
- Roman numerals give the charge of metals that form more than one ion: iron(II), iron(III), copper(II).
- -ide: one element (chloride, oxide). -ate: polyatomic ion with oxygen (sulfate, nitrate).
- Write ionic formulas so the charges add to zero; bracket a polyatomic ion used more than once.
- Non-metals only: **molecular**. Use mono, di, tri, tetra...; no "mono" on the first element.
</RememberThis>
