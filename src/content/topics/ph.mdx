## What is it?

**pH** is a number that tells you how acidic or basic a solution is. It is based on the concentration of hydronium ions, $\ce{H3O+}$:

$$
\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]
$$

where $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is the concentration in mol/L.

At 25 °C:

- **pH < 7**: acidic
- **pH = 7**: neutral
- **pH > 7**: basic (also called alkaline)

<KeyIdea>
The pH scale is **logarithmic**: each change of 1 pH unit means a **10-fold** change in $[\ce{H3O+}]$. A solution at pH 3 has 1000 times more $\ce{H3O+}$ than one at pH 6.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Living things depend on it.** Human blood is kept between about pH 7.35 and 7.45; a change of a few tenths can be dangerous.
- **Chemistry depends on it.** The speed and outcome of many reactions, and how well many substances dissolve, change with pH.
- **Everyday life depends on it.** Soil pH affects which crops grow, swimming pools and drinking water are kept within set pH ranges, and many food and cleaning products are designed around their pH.

## How does it work?

### 1. Water always contains a little H₃O⁺ and OH⁻

Water reacts with itself very slightly:

$$
\ce{2H2O <=> H3O+ + OH-}
$$

At 25 °C, the product of the two concentrations is always the same constant, $K_\text{w}$:

$$
K_\text{w} = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
$$

In pure water both concentrations are $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M, so the pH is 7.00. Adding acid raises $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and lowers $[\ce{OH-}]$, and adding base does the opposite.

### 2. pOH and the link between pH and pOH

$$
\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]
$$

and, at 25 °C,

$$
\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00
$$

### 3. Strong acids and bases

A strong acid ionizes completely, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals the acid's concentration (for acids that give one $\ce{H+}$, such as HCl and HNO₃). Likewise, for a strong base, $[\ce{OH-}]$ comes straight from its concentration, **remembering how many OH⁻ each formula unit releases** (2 for $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$).

### 4. Going back from pH to concentration

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}
$$

<Analogy>
Think of prices that each have one more zero: 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000. Each step is ten times the last. The pH scale counts those zeros: each step **down** in pH means ten times more H₃O⁺. So pH 4 isn't twice as acidic as pH 8. It has **10,000 times** more H₃O⁺.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
$K_\text{w}$ changes with temperature, so neutral water has pH 7.00 only at 25 °C (at higher temperatures, neutral pH is a little lower). pH can also be below 0 or above 14 for very concentrated acids or bases. For very dilute acids, the $\ce{H3O+}$ from water itself can't be ignored: $1 \times 10^{-8}$ M HCl is slightly acidic, not pH 8. Strictly, pH is defined using *activity* rather than concentration, but concentration works well for dilute solutions.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="pH of a strong acid">
**Question:** What is the pH of 0.050 M hydrochloric acid?

1. HCl is a strong acid, so $[\ce{H3O+}] = 0.050$ M.
2. $\text{pH} = -\log(0.050) = 1.30$
</Example>

<Example title="pH of a strong base">
**Question:** What is the pH of 0.020 M calcium hydroxide, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$?

1. Each $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ releases **two** $\ce{OH-}$ ions: $[\ce{OH-}] = 2 \times 0.020 = 0.040$ M
2. $\text{pOH} = -\log(0.040) = 1.40$
3. $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 1.40 = 12.60$
</Example>

<Example title="Concentration from pH">
**Question:** A solution has pH 4.50. What is $[\ce{H3O+}]$?

$[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-4.50} = 3.2 \times 10^{-5}$ M
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Thinking the pH scale is linear">
pH 4 is **not** twice as acidic as pH 8. Each pH unit is a factor of 10, so the difference is $10^4$ = 10,000 times.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Forgetting the number of OH⁻ ions">
For $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ or $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, $[\ce{OH-}]$ is **twice** the concentration of the base. Using 0.020 M instead of 0.040 M in the example gives pH 12.30 instead of 12.60.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Mixing up pH and pOH">
For a base, you usually calculate **pOH** first from $[\ce{OH-}]$, then convert: pH = 14.00 − pOH. Reporting the pOH as the pH is a very common slip.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- $[\ce{H+}]$ and $[\ce{H3O+}]$ are used interchangeably in pH calculations.
- **Significant figures:** the number of decimal places in a pH equals the number of significant figures in the concentration. 0.050 M (2 s.f.) gives pH 1.30 (2 decimal places).
- "log" here means the base-10 logarithm (the "log" key on a calculator, not "ln").
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- pH = −log[H₃O⁺]; [H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
- At 25 °C: pH < 7 acidic, = 7 neutral, > 7 basic; pH + pOH = 14.00.
- 1 pH unit = 10× change in [H₃O⁺].
- Strong acids/bases: concentration of H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ comes straight from the formula (count the OH⁻!).
</RememberThis>
