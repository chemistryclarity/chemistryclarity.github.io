## What is it?

**Le Chatelier's principle:** if a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in concentration, pressure or temperature, the position of equilibrium shifts in the direction that **partly counteracts** the change.

"Shifts to the right" means more products form; "shifts to the left" means more reactants form. The system never fully undoes the change; it only reduces its effect.

<KeyIdea>
Changes in concentration and pressure shift the equilibrium **without changing K**: they make $Q \ne K$, and the reaction runs until $Q = K$ again. A change in **temperature changes K itself**. A catalyst changes neither.
</KeyIdea>

## Why does it matter?

- **Industry.** Chemists choose temperatures and pressures that push equilibria toward the product, as in the Haber process for ammonia.
- **Everyday chemistry.** A fizzy drink goes flat when opened because lowering the pressure shifts $\ce{CO2(aq) <=> CO2(g)}$ toward the gas.
- **Living systems.** The body's buffers respond to added acid or base following the same principle.

## How does it work?

### 1. Changing a concentration

- **Add** a reactant (or remove a product): $Q$ becomes smaller than $K$, so the equilibrium shifts **right** to use some of it up.
- **Add** a product (or remove a reactant): $Q > K$, so it shifts **left**.

### 2. Changing the pressure (gases)

Increasing the pressure by **reducing the volume** shifts the equilibrium toward the side with **fewer moles of gas**, which lowers the pressure:

$$
\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}
$$

The left side has 1 mol + 3 mol = 4 mol of gas; the right side has 2 mol.

Higher pressure shifts this equilibrium **right**, toward ammonia. If both sides have the same number of moles of gas (as in $\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}$), pressure has no effect on the position. Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not change any concentration, so it has no effect either.

### 3. Changing the temperature

Treat heat as a reactant or product:

- **Exothermic** forward reaction (ΔH negative): heat is a "product". Raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium **left** and **decreases K**.
- **Endothermic** forward reaction (ΔH positive): heat is a "reactant". Raising the temperature shifts it **right** and **increases K**.

For $\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$, ΔH = +57 kJ: on heating, more brown $\ce{NO2}$ forms, so the colourless-to-brown mixture gets **darker**.

### 4. Adding a catalyst

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions **equally**. Equilibrium is reached **sooner**, but its position and $K$ are unchanged.

| Change | Effect on position | Effect on K |
|---|---|---|
| Add reactant / remove product | shifts right | none |
| Add product / remove reactant | shifts left | none |
| Increase pressure (smaller volume) | toward fewer moles of gas | none |
| Increase temperature | in the endothermic direction | changes |
| Add a catalyst | none (equilibrium reached faster) | none |

<Analogy>
Think of a crowded room with two doors and people moving between it and the corridor at equal rates. Push more people into the room and, for a while, more people leave than enter, until the flows balance again. The room ends up a bit more crowded than before, but less crowded than right after the push: the change is partly counteracted.
</Analogy>

<MorePrecisely>
Le Chatelier's principle predicts the **direction** of a shift; comparing $Q$ with $K$ explains **why**, and an ICE table gives the **new concentrations**. How strongly $K$ depends on temperature is described by the van 't Hoff equation, which links it to ΔH°.
</MorePrecisely>

## Worked example

<Example title="Predicting shifts in the Haber process">
**Question:** For $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$, ΔH = −92 kJ, predict the effect on the amount of ammonia of (a) adding $\ce{N2}$ (b) increasing the pressure (c) increasing the temperature (d) adding an iron catalyst.

1. Adding $\ce{N2}$ (a reactant): shifts **right**, so **more** $\ce{NH3}$.
2. Higher pressure: 4 mol gas on the left, 2 mol on the right, so it shifts toward fewer moles: **right**, **more** $\ce{NH3}$.
3. The forward reaction is exothermic, so heating shifts **left**: **less** $\ce{NH3}$ (and $K$ decreases).
4. A catalyst does not shift the equilibrium: **no change** in the amount, but equilibrium is reached faster.
</Example>

<Example title="Checking a shift with Q">
**Question:** An equilibrium mixture of $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$ ($K_\text{c} = 49.0$) contains 0.0222 M $\ce{H2}$, 0.0222 M $\ce{I2}$ and 0.156 M HI. 0.100 M $\ce{H2}$ is added. Which way does the equilibrium shift?

1. Just after the addition, $[\ce{H2}] = 0.0222\ \text{M} + 0.100\ \text{M} = 0.122\ \text{M}$
2. $Q = \dfrac{(0.156\ \text{M})^2}{(0.122\ \text{M})(0.0222\ \text{M})} = 8.99$ (the units of M² cancel)
3. $Q = 8.99 < K_\text{c} = 49.0$, so the reaction goes **forward**: $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{I2}$ are used up and more HI forms, as Le Chatelier's principle predicts.
</Example>

## Common mistake

<CommonMistake title="Thinking concentration changes alter K">
Adding a reactant shifts the **position**, but $K$ stays the same. Only temperature changes $K$.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Thinking a catalyst increases the yield">
A catalyst makes equilibrium arrive faster but does not change how much product is present at equilibrium.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Counting all moles, not moles of gas">
For pressure changes, count only **gases**. In $\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$ there is 0 mol of gas on the left and 1 mol on the right.
</CommonMistake>

<NotationNote>
- "Shifts to the right / forward" = more products; "to the left / backward" = more reactants.
- ΔH refers to the forward reaction as written; the reverse reaction has the opposite sign.
</NotationNote>

## Remember this

<RememberThis>
- A disturbed equilibrium shifts to partly counteract the change.
- Add reactant or remove product: right. Higher pressure: fewer moles of gas.
- Heating favours the endothermic direction and is the only change that alters $K$.
- A catalyst gives equilibrium faster but does not move it.
</RememberThis>
