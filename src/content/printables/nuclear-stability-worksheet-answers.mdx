## A. Ideas

<Q n={1}>The strong force.</Q>
<Answer>(a) A very **short-range attraction** between all nucleons (protons and neutrons). (b) Protons repel each other across the whole nucleus, but the strong force acts only between neighbours. Extra **neutrons add attraction without adding repulsion**, so larger nuclei need more of them. (c) Beyond Z = 83, the total proton repulsion is too great for any number of neutrons to hold the nucleus together permanently.</Answer>

<Q n={2}>N/Z.</Q>
<Answer>(a) 2/2 = **1.00** (b) 8/8 = **1.00** (c) 30/26 = **1.15** (d) 124/82 = **1.51**. The ratio **rises** with atomic number: heavier stable nuclei need proportionally more neutrons.</Answer>

## B. Predicting decay

<Q n={3}>Decay equations.</Q>
<Answer>

(a) Co-60 has one more neutron than stable Co-59: **β⁻**. $\ce{^{60}_{27}Co -> ^{60}_{28}Ni + ^{0}_{-1}e}$

(b) F-18 has one fewer neutron than stable F-19: **β⁺**. $\ce{^{18}_{9}F -> ^{18}_{8}O + ^{0}_{+1}e}$

(c) Rn-222 has Z = 86, beyond 83: **α**. $\ce{^{222}_{86}Rn -> ^{218}_{84}Po + ^{4}_{2}He}$

(d) P-32 has one more neutron than stable P-31: **β⁻**. $\ce{^{32}_{15}P -> ^{32}_{16}S + ^{0}_{-1}e}$

</Answer>

<Q n={4}>Doubly magic.</Q>
<Answer>Both the number of protons **and** the number of neutrons are magic numbers: Ca-40 has 20 and 20; Pb-208 has 82 and 126. Like full electron shells, full nuclear shells give **extra stability**, so these nuclides are especially stable (Pb-208 is the end of the thorium decay series).</Answer>

## C. Decay series

<Q n={5}>Th-232 to Pb-208.</Q>
<Answer>α: (232 − 208) ÷ 4 = **6**. Six α lower Z by 12: 90 − 12 = 78. Lead is 82, so Z rises by 4: **4 β⁻**. Check: 90 − 12 + 4 = 82 ✓</Answer>

<Q n={6}>U-235 to Pb-207.</Q>
<Answer>α: (235 − 207) ÷ 4 = **7**. Seven α lower Z by 14: 92 − 14 = 78. Lead is 82: **4 β⁻**. Check: 92 − 14 + 4 = 82 ✓</Answer>

## D. Applying the ideas

<Q n={7}>Fluorine-18.</Q>
<Answer>F-18 has N/Z = 9/9 = 1.00, lower than stable F-19 (10/9 = 1.11): it has **too few neutrons**, so it lies below the band. It converts a proton into a neutron by **emitting a positron**, which then meets an electron in the body and produces the gamma rays a PET scanner detects.</Answer>

## E. Challenge

<Q n={8}>Tritium.</Q>
<Answer>Tritium has 1 proton and 2 neutrons: N/Z = 2/1 = **2.0**, far above the stable ratios for hydrogen (0 and 1). Too many neutrons: **β⁻ decay**. $\ce{^{3}_{1}H -> ^{3}_{2}He + ^{0}_{-1}e}$</Answer>
